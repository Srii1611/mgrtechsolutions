Your website doesn't send you a complaint when it fails. It just sits there while potential customers hit the back button and call your competitor — and you'd never know it happened. The good news: the ways local business websites lose customers follow predictable patterns, and you can check every one of them yourself in under an hour. Here's your inspection guide.

## Why This Matters More Than You Think

Most people who visit a local service website are already warm leads. They searched for what you do, in your area, and clicked your name. Losing them at that point is like having a customer walk into your shop, look around for ten seconds, and walk out because nobody acknowledged them. You paid — in ad money, SEO effort, or years of reputation — to get them to the door. These checks find the places where they're slipping back out.

## Check #1: The Phone Test (Your Most Important One)

Most local service customers will visit your site on a phone, often in a hurry. Pull out your own phone right now and load your site. Ask:

- **Does it load in about three seconds or less?** If you're staring at a spinner, so are your customers — and a large share of them leave rather than wait.
- **Is your phone number visible at the top without scrolling?** And can you tap it to call?
- **Can you read everything without pinching and zooming?** Tiny text and sideways-scrolling pages are instant exit triggers.
- **Do buttons work with a thumb?** Cramped links that mis-tap frustrate people into leaving.

If your site fails any of these on mobile, it's failing the majority of your visitors. This alone is the most common revenue leak in local business websites.

## Check #2: The Five-Second Test

Show your homepage to a friend or family member for five seconds, then close it. Ask them three questions:

1. What does this business do?
2. Where does it do it?
3. How would you contact them?

If they can't answer all three instantly, your homepage is losing people. Visitors decide within seconds whether they're in the right place. A vague headline ("Excellence in Every Project") tells them nothing; "Water Heater Repair & Installation in Springfield" tells them everything.

## Check #3: The Contact Path Audit

Pretend you're a customer and try to contact yourself. Count the obstacles:

- **Is the phone number on every page?** Customers shouldn't have to hunt for a Contact page.
- **Does the contact form actually work?** Submit a test. Then check where it went — many businesses discover months of leads sitting in a spam folder or an inbox nobody monitors.
- **How many fields does your form have?** Every extra field (full address, "how did you hear about us," dropdown menus) shrinks the number of people who finish it. Name, phone, and "what do you need" is enough.
- **Is there any response promise?** "We reply within one business hour" reassures people their message won't vanish into a void.

## Check #4: The Trust Scan

Customers hiring someone to come into their home are looking for reasons to trust you — or reasons not to. Check whether your site provides:

- **Reviews or testimonials** with real first names and towns (not anonymous praise)
- **Real photos** of your team, trucks, and completed work — not stock photos of smiling models with wrenches
- **License and insurance info**, if your trade requires it
- **A named human.** An About page with your face and a sentence about why you do this work outperforms corporate-speak every time
- **Your service area in plain text** — a list of towns you serve, not just a map graphic

A site that could belong to any company in any city quietly pushes visitors toward competitors who feel local and real.

## Check #5: The Staleness Check

An outdated site signals an outdated business. Look for:

- A copyright date from years ago in the footer
- "News" or blog sections whose latest post is two years old
- Services listed that you no longer offer (or missing services you've added)
- Old team photos featuring people who left
- Seasonal banners for a holiday that passed months ago

Each one plants a small doubt: "Are these guys still in business?"

## Check #6: The Search Visibility Spot-Check

Some customer loss happens before your site even gets a chance. Try these:

- **Google your main service plus your town** ("furnace repair Springfield"). Do you appear on page one, in the map results?
- **Search your own business name.** Does your website show up first, with your Google Business Profile beside it? If searching your *name* doesn't find you cleanly, something's wrong.
- **Look at your listing from the customer's view.** Wrong hours, an old phone number, or no reviews on your profile all suppress calls before your website is ever seen.

## Check #7: The Data Check (If You Have Analytics)

If your site has Google Analytics or a similar tool connected, two numbers tell the story:

- **Bounce/engagement on key pages:** if most visitors leave your homepage within seconds, it's failing the five-second test at scale.
- **Traffic vs. inquiries:** if your site gets visitors but your phone doesn't ring proportionally, the problem is the site, not your marketing. (If you have no analytics installed, that itself is a fix-it item — you can't spot leaks you can't see.)

## Your Triage Plan: Fix in This Order

If you found problems, don't try to fix everything at once. Prioritize by how directly each issue blocks a customer from contacting you:

1. **Broken contact paths** — dead forms, wrong numbers, unmonitored inboxes (fix today)
2. **Mobile experience** — slow load, no tap-to-call, unreadable text (this week)
3. **Homepage clarity** — what you do, where, and how to reach you, above the fold (this week)
4. **Trust signals** — reviews, real photos, service area (this month)
5. **Freshness** — update dates, remove stale content, add recent project photos (this month)

Even fixing the top two often produces a noticeable change in call volume, because you're no longer losing the people your marketing already delivered.

## Quick Checklist

- [ ] I loaded my site on my phone: fast, readable, tap-to-call number at the top
- [ ] A stranger could tell what I do, where, and how to contact me within five seconds
- [ ] I submitted a test form and confirmed it reaches an inbox someone monitors
- [ ] My form asks only for name, phone, and what's needed
- [ ] My site shows real reviews, real photos, license info, and my service area in text
- [ ] Nothing on the site is stale: dates, old services, ex-employee photos
- [ ] I searched "[my service] + [my town]" and my business name — I show up cleanly
- [ ] Analytics are installed so I can see visitor behavior going forward
- [ ] I've made a prioritized fix list, starting with contact paths and mobile
