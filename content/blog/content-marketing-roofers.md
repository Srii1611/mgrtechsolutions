Roofing has a content problem: most roofing websites say the same five things ("licensed and insured," "free estimates," "quality workmanship") and then wonder why nobody reads their blog. Meanwhile, homeowners are searching thousands of specific questions about leaks, insurance claims, and shingle life spans — questions you're perfectly positioned to answer.

Writing about the right topics turns your website from a digital business card into a lead-generating asset. Here's what to write about, how often, and how to make sure readers actually pick up the phone.

## Why Content Matters More in Roofing Than Most Trades

A roof replacement is one of the biggest checks a homeowner writes — often five figures — and it's something they do maybe twice in their life. That combination of high cost and low familiarity creates *anxiety*, and anxious people research. They read reviews, compare materials, and look for contractors who explain things clearly instead of talking down to them.

Roofing also has a trust deficit. Storm-chasers and fly-by-night crews have made homeowners cautious, especially after hail or hurricane events. A library of genuinely helpful articles signals something a yard sign never can: you've been here, you know your trade, and you'll still be here in five years.

## What to Actually Write About: Seven Topic Categories

### 1. "Do I Need a Repair or a Replacement?"

This is the single most valuable question in roofing content because it's what every homeowner with a problem wants to know before calling anyone. Write pieces like:

- "Roof Leak: When a Repair Is Enough and When It's Throwing Money Away"
- "My Roof Is 18 Years Old — How Much Longer Does It Have?"
- "Missing Shingles After a Storm: Patch It or Replace the Slope?"

Be honest in these articles, including the cases where a cheap repair is the right answer. That honesty is what makes people trust you when the answer *is* replacement.

### 2. Storm Damage and Insurance Claims

After any significant storm, searches for hail and wind damage spike in your area. Be the contractor who explains the process calmly:

- "What Hail Damage Actually Looks Like (With Photos)"
- "How the Roof Insurance Claim Process Works, Step by Step"
- "Should I Call My Roofer or My Insurance Company First?"

A word of caution: explain the process, don't promise outcomes. Never suggest claims will be approved or that you'll "handle the insurance company" in ways that overstep. Helpful, factual, and compliant wins.

### 3. Material Comparisons

Homeowners know the word "shingle" and little else. Material content positions you as an educator:

- "Architectural vs. 3-Tab Shingles: Is the Upgrade Worth It?"
- "Metal Roofing: Who It's Right For (and Who Should Skip It)"
- "What Those Roofing Warranties Actually Cover"

Include the downsides of every option — including ones you sell. Readers can smell a sales pitch; balanced comparisons build the trust that gets you the estimate request.

### 4. Cost and Pricing Transparency

You don't need to publish your prices, but you should explain what drives them:

- "Why Roofing Quotes Vary So Much Between Contractors"
- "The 6 Things That Determine What a New Roof Costs"
- "What a 'Cheap' Roof Really Costs You Over 20 Years"

Price-focused readers are close to buying. An article that explains cost factors — roof pitch, layers to tear off, decking condition, ventilation — pre-educates your leads so your estimate appointments go smoother.

### 5. Maintenance and Prevention

Not every article should target people who need a roof now. Maintenance content builds an audience of homeowners who will need one eventually:

- "The 15-Minute Roof Check Every Homeowner Should Do Each Fall"
- "How Clogged Gutters Quietly Destroy Your Roof Edge"
- "Attic Ventilation: The Roof Problem Nobody Looks At"

These readers may not call this month, but when their roof fails in two years, they'll remember who taught them to spot the warning signs.

### 6. Project Walkthroughs and Photo Stories

Roofing is visual, and your job photos are an unfair advantage. After notable jobs, write a short walkthrough: what the problem was, what you found during tear-off, what you installed, and how long it took. Mention the neighborhood or town (with the customer's permission) — "a 1970s ranch in [town]" — because local detail helps you show up in local searches.

### 7. Buyer-Protection Content

Given the industry's reputation problem, articles that teach homeowners how to hire well paradoxically win you business:

- "7 Questions to Ask Before Hiring a Roofing Contractor"
- "Red Flags After a Storm: How to Spot a Storm-Chaser"
- "Why the Lowest Roofing Bid Is Rarely the Best Value"

When your own company meets every criterion on your checklist, the article sells without selling.

## A Simple System for Keeping It Going

The biggest failure mode is stopping. Here's a sustainable approach:

1. **Mine your phone calls.** Every question a customer or adjuster asks you is a topic. Keep a note in your phone and add to it weekly.
2. **Publish twice a month.** That's it. Two solid articles a month beats a flurry followed by silence, and it's a pace that survives your busy season.
3. **Time storm content fast, seasonal content early.** Storm articles should go up within days of the event; seasonal prep articles should publish one to two months before the season.
4. **Use your own photos.** Real job-site photos outperform stock images with readers and search engines alike.
5. **Repurpose everything.** Each article becomes a Facebook post, a short video script, and a few Google Business Profile updates.

## Making Content Convert Into Estimates

Readers don't become leads by accident. Every article needs:

- **One clear next step** — "Get a free roof inspection" or "Schedule an estimate" — stated plainly near the end, with a phone number and a short form.
- **Your service area in the text.** "We inspect roofs across [county/towns]" tells both readers and search engines where you work.
- **Internal links.** Your hail-damage article should link to your insurance-claims article, which should link to your contact page. Guide the reader down a path.
- **Proof near the ask.** A sentence like "We've replaced 40+ roofs in [town] in the past two years" next to your call to action reassures people right before they act.

## Setting Realistic Expectations

Content marketing is not a switch you flip. Many contractors find it takes several months of consistent publishing before articles start ranking and generating calls, and the biggest payoff comes in year two and beyond, as your older articles keep working while you add new ones. Think of it like building a reputation in a new town — slow at first, then self-sustaining.

The roofers who win at this aren't better writers; they're the ones who kept answering real customer questions long after their competitors gave up.
