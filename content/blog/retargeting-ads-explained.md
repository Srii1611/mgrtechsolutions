Ever looked at a product online and then seen ads for it everywhere for the next week? That's retargeting — and it's not just for big online stores. Local service businesses can use it too, often cheaply, to stay in front of people who visited their website but didn't call. Here's how it works, whether it makes sense for your business, and how to do it without wasting money or creeping people out.

## What Retargeting Actually Is

Most visitors to your website leave without contacting you. That's normal — homeowners get quotes from multiple companies, get interrupted, or simply aren't ready to decide. Without retargeting, those visitors are gone for good.

Retargeting (also called remarketing) lets you show ads specifically to people who already visited your site. Here's the mechanics in plain terms:

1. You place a small piece of code — called a pixel or tag — on your website. Ad platforms like Google, Facebook, and Instagram provide it.
2. When someone visits your site, the pixel quietly notes the visit (anonymously — you don't get their name).
3. Later, as that person browses other websites, watches YouTube, or scrolls Facebook, your ads can appear to them.
4. They click, come back, and — this time — call or fill out your form.

The core idea: you're advertising to people who already know you exist and have already shown interest. That's a much warmer audience than strangers, which is why retargeting ads are usually cheaper per result than cold advertising.

## Why It's a Natural Fit for Service Businesses

Think about how homeowners hire. Someone with a broken furnace in January calls the first company that answers — that's a search, not a browsing decision. But for bigger, planned jobs — a roof replacement, a kitchen remodel, new fencing, landscaping — people research for days or weeks. They visit several sites, compare, get distracted, and circle back.

During that research window, retargeting keeps your name in front of them. When they're finally ready to request quotes, you're the company they've "seen everywhere" — which feels a lot like being the established, trustworthy choice. Familiarity is a genuine competitive advantage in a trust-based purchase.

## The Honest Downsides

Retargeting isn't magic, and it's not for everyone:

- **It needs traffic to work.** The pixel builds an audience from your visitors. If your site gets very little traffic, there's almost nobody to retarget. Build traffic first (Google Business Profile, reviews, content) before spending here.
- **Emergency services benefit less.** A homeowner with a burst pipe hires within the hour. There's no research window to retarget through. Retargeting pays off most for considered, higher-ticket jobs.
- **It can feel creepy if overdone.** Ads that follow someone for months, or that say things implying you know what they looked at, breed resentment rather than trust. Frequency and duration limits solve most of this.
- **Privacy rules apply.** Depending on your location and your visitors' locations, you may need a cookie notice on your site and a privacy policy that discloses tracking. Keep your privacy policy current and, when in doubt, disclose.

## Where to Run Retargeting

The two main options for a local business:

- **Facebook and Instagram (Meta).** The Meta pixel tracks your visitors, and your ads appear in their feeds. Cheap per impression, visual (great for showing off finished work), and most homeowners are there. The natural starting point for most local businesses.
- **Google (display and YouTube).** Your ads appear as banners on other websites your visitors browse, or before YouTube videos. Broader reach but easier to waste money on irrelevant placements if you're not careful with settings.

Start with one platform, learn it, then expand if the numbers justify it.

## Setting Up a Campaign That Doesn't Waste Money

You don't need to become an ads expert, but a few decisions matter a lot.

### 1. Target only your service area

This is the mistake that burns the most money. Set your ads to show only within the towns or zip codes you actually serve. Otherwise you'll pay to retarget visitors from three states away who will never hire you.

### 2. Segment by page if you can

Someone who visited your "roof replacement" page has a different need than someone who read a blog post about gutters. Most platforms let you build audiences from specific pages and show each group a matching ad. Even two or three segments beats one generic campaign.

### 3. Limit frequency and duration

Cap how often one person sees your ad (a handful of times per week is plenty) and how long you keep following them. For most services, 30 to 90 days covers the decision window. Someone who visited your site eight months ago and never came back is not coming back.

### 4. Use your best real photos

Retargeting ads work because of recognition: your truck, your logo, your completed jobs. A striking photo of a finished project with your branding beats generic stock imagery every time.

### 5. Send clicks to the right page

The ad should land on the page matching the service — not your homepage. The person already knows who you are; make it one click to call or request a quote.

### 6. Exclude people who already converted

Someone who already submitted your form shouldn't keep seeing your ads. Most platforms let you exclude them (by tracking the thank-you page, for example). It saves money and avoids annoying people who are already customers.

## What Should You Spend?

Retargeting audiences for a local business are small — often a few hundred to a few thousand people — so budgets stay modest. Many local businesses run effective retargeting on a few dollars a day. The real question isn't the budget but the return: track how many calls and form fills come from retargeting, and compare cost per lead against your other channels. If a retargeted lead costs less than a lead you're buying elsewhere, it's earning its keep.

Give a new campaign at least a month before judging it, and don't judge on clicks alone — judge on leads and booked jobs.

## Where Retargeting Fits in Your Marketing

Retargeting is a closer, not an opener. It doesn't create demand or bring in new visitors; it recovers visitors you already earned through your website, your Google presence, and your other marketing. That means the priority order for most local businesses is: get found and get traffic first, make sure your website converts, then add retargeting to scoop up the ones who got away.

## Quick Checklist

- [ ] Website has enough traffic to build a retargeting audience
- [ ] Service suits it (considered, higher-ticket jobs more than emergencies)
- [ ] Pixel installed on every page of the website
- [ ] Privacy policy updated and cookie notice added where required
- [ ] Ads limited to your actual service area
- [ ] Audiences segmented by page or service where possible
- [ ] Frequency capped so nobody sees your ad constantly
- [ ] Retargeting window set to a sensible 30–90 days
- [ ] Real job photos and clear branding in the ads
- [ ] Ad clicks land on the matching service page
- [ ] Past converters excluded from seeing ads
- [ ] Cost per lead tracked and compared against other channels
