Ask five web companies what a contractor website costs and you'll get five wildly different answers — anywhere from "free, build it tonight" to $25,000. Both extremes can be the wrong answer for the same business. The truth is that "a website" isn't one product; it's a range of options that suit different stages of a business, and knowing what each tier actually buys you is the only way to avoid overpaying or underbuying.

Here's a plain-English breakdown of the real price ranges in 2026, what's included at each level, the ongoing costs nobody mentions upfront, and how to decide what's right for where your business is now.

## The Four Realistic Tiers

### Tier 1: DIY Website Builders — $0 to $50/Month

Tools like Squarespace, Wix, and similar builders let you put together a serviceable site yourself for the cost of a monthly subscription plus your time.

**What you get:** A template-based site with your logo, photos, service descriptions, and a contact form. Modern builders are genuinely decent-looking.

**What you don't get:** Strategy, custom copywriting, local SEO structure, or anyone to call when something breaks. The real cost is your evenings — expect 20 to 40 hours to do it reasonably well.

**Who it's right for:** Brand-new businesses in their first year, side hustles, or anyone whose budget is truly zero. A clean DIY site beats no site by a mile. Plan to upgrade once revenue allows.

### Tier 2: Freelancer or Small Studio — $1,500 to $5,000 One-Time

A freelancer builds you a custom-ish site, usually on WordPress or a similar platform, based on your content and direction.

**What you get:** A professional design tailored to your brand, your core pages (home, services, about, contact), basic mobile responsiveness, and a contact form. Some include basic on-page SEO like title tags and page structure — ask explicitly, because many don't.

**What you don't get (usually):** Written content (you'll supply the words), photography, deep local SEO work, or ongoing marketing. Once it launches, maintenance is on you unless you pay separately.

**Watch out for:** Rock-bottom freelancers who disappear after launch, leaving you with a site you can't update. Always confirm you own the domain, hosting account, and site files — in your name, on your accounts.

### Tier 3: Small Agency Website — $5,000 to $15,000

This is the sweet spot for established local service businesses doing, say, $500K to several million in revenue, where the website is a real lead-generation asset rather than a brochure.

**What you should get:** Strategy before design (who your customers are, what they search for), professional copywriting, one page per service plus location pages, proper local SEO structure, fast load times, mobile-first design, photo guidance or a photo shoot, review integration, and call tracking or form tracking so you can measure leads.

**What to verify:** Whether content writing is included (it's often the line item that separates a $5K quote from a $10K one), who owns the site if you leave, and what ongoing maintenance costs.

### Tier 4: Performance or Growth-Focused Builds — $15,000 to $30,000+

For larger operations — multi-crew companies, multi-location businesses, or firms in cutthroat metro markets — the website becomes a bigger system: dozens of service and location pages, booking integrations, financing calculators, career pages for recruiting, and deep analytics. Sometimes this tier bundles an ongoing SEO and content program with the build.

This tier only makes sense when your lead volume and job values justify it. A two-person plumbing shop does not need a $25,000 website; a $5M HVAC company in a big metro probably does.

## The Ongoing Costs Nobody Puts in the Brochure

The build price is only part of the story. Budget for these recurring items:

| Item | Typical cost | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| Domain name | $10–20/year | Your web address — always register it yourself |
| Hosting | $10–50/month | Where your site lives; cheap hosting is a common cause of slow sites |
| Maintenance/security | $50–200/month | Updates, backups, fixes — or DIY it and risk hacks |
| Content/SEO (optional) | $500–2,500/month | New pages, blog posts, review strategy — the stuff that actually grows traffic |

Two rules of thumb: never let an agency hold your domain or hosting hostage in their accounts, and be clear whether your "website package" is a one-time build or a lease. Some companies rent you a website — stop paying and the site vanishes. That model isn't inherently bad, but you should know which one you're signing.

## How to Decide What You Actually Need

Ask yourself three questions:

1. **What's a new customer worth?** If your average job is $400 and your site brings two extra jobs a month, even a modest site pays for itself. If your average job is $15,000 (roof replacements, remodels), one extra closed job pays for a serious website several times over.
2. **How competitive is your market?** In a small town, a clean DIY site and a strong Google Business Profile may be plenty. In a competitive metro, a thin site won't get seen, and the money is better spent on a proper build plus ongoing content.
3. **Where is your business today?** Match the investment to your stage. Pre-revenue? DIY. Established and growing? Tier 2 or 3. Multi-crew and scaling? Tier 3 or 4 with ongoing support.

## Getting a Quote Without Getting Burned

When you're comparing proposals, insist on answers to these questions:

- Is copywriting included, or do I write the content?
- Do I own the domain, hosting, and website files?
- Is the site built to load fast on phones, and will you show me speed test results?
- What does it cost to make changes after launch?
- Is local SEO structure (service pages, location pages, proper page titles) included or extra?
- Can I see three sites you've built for similar businesses — and can I call those owners?

A trustworthy provider answers these directly. Vague answers about ownership or post-launch costs are a reason to walk away.

## The Bottom Line

For most established local service businesses in 2026, the honest range is **$3,000 to $10,000 for a quality build, plus $100 to $300 a month to keep it healthy** — more if you add ongoing content and SEO. Below that, you're trading your own time or accepting limitations; above that, make sure the extra spend maps to specific revenue outcomes, not just fancier design.

A website isn't a cost to minimize; it's a salesperson that works around the clock. The right question isn't "what's the cheapest site I can get?" — it's "what will this site need to earn back, and can it?"
