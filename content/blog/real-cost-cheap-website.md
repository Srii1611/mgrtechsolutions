When you're running a service business, a cheap website is tempting. A few hundred dollars — or a free do-it-yourself builder — and you're "online." Box checked. But the real price of a website isn't what you pay to build it. It's what it costs you, month after month, in customers who never call. When you add that up, the bargain site is often the most expensive option on the table.

## The Math Nobody Does

Here's the calculation that changes how most owners think about this. Say your average job is worth $400 — a typical figure for many trades. If your website is supposed to bring in ten jobs a month but a slow, confusing, hard-to-find site only produces four, the six missing jobs cost you $2,400 a month. That's nearly $29,000 a year — for a website you saved maybe $2,000 on.

Higher-ticket businesses feel it harder. A remodeler whose average project is $40,000 doesn't need to lose many leads for a cheap website to become the most expensive thing they own.

The point isn't that expensive websites are automatically good — plenty aren't. It's that a website should be evaluated on what it produces, not what it costs to buy.

## Where Cheap Websites Bleed Money

### They load slowly — and visitors leave

Budget sites are usually built on bloated templates, stuffed with oversized photos, and hosted on the cheapest servers available. The result is a site that takes five, six, eight seconds to load on a phone. Meanwhile, a large share of your potential customers are searching on cellular connections, often mid-emergency. Many will hit the back button before your homepage even appears — and they'll never know how good your work is.

### They're invisible in search

Cheap builds almost never include the things that make a site rank: individual pages for each service, proper page titles and headings, location-specific content, fast load times, clean structure. A site that doesn't rank is a brochure nobody sees — which means you're paying for a website and *still* paying for every single customer through ads or lead services, forever.

### They don't convert the visitors they do get

Conversion is where budget sites suffer most visibly:

- Phone numbers buried on a contact page instead of tappable on every screen
- No clear reason to trust you — no reviews, no real photos, no credentials
- Generic stock imagery that makes you indistinguishable from everyone else
- Forms with twelve fields, or broken forms, or forms that email an inbox nobody checks
- No clear next step anywhere on the page

A site that gets 500 visitors a month and converts 2% produces ten leads. The same site, done properly, might convert 6% — thirty leads from identical traffic. The difference isn't more marketing; it's a website that does its job.

### They look like a risk

Fairly or not, customers judge your business by your website — especially for high-trust, high-ticket work done inside their homes. A dated design, broken mobile layout, blurry logo, or a copyright notice from 2016 whispers "this company doesn't pay attention to details." For a contractor asking a homeowner to trust them with a $30,000 renovation, that whisper is fatal. The customer can't see your craftsmanship yet; the website is standing in for it.

### They cost more to maintain than to replace

Cheap sites have a way of generating ongoing invoices: the plugin that broke, the contact form that silently stopped sending emails (how many leads did you miss before you noticed?), the page builder update that scrambled the layout, the hack that got the site flagged. Owners of bargain sites often pay for the site twice — once to build it, and again in drips of emergency fixes and lost leads.

## The Hidden Costs Nobody Mentions

- **Your own time.** Hours spent fighting a do-it-yourself builder are hours not spent running jobs. Your time has a rate too.
- **Lock-in.** Some cheap platforms make it painful or impossible to take your site with you. Want to leave? Start over.
- **No ownership of the relationship.** Some low-cost providers effectively hold your domain, your content, or your listings hostage. Always confirm: you own the domain, you own the content, and you have admin access to everything.
- **Missed compounding.** A good website builds value over time — rankings, reviews, content, authority. Every year spent on a site that can't rank is a year of compounding you don't get back.

## What a Good Website Actually Requires

You don't need a $20,000 custom build. For most local service businesses, an effective website needs:

- **Fast loading** on a phone, with properly optimized images
- **One page per service**, written for customers, in plain language
- **A tappable phone number and a short quote form** visible everywhere
- **Real photos of your work and team** — not stock models
- **Reviews, license info, and guarantees** displayed where decisions happen
- **Accurate, consistent business information** that matches your listings
- **Basic search engine structure** so you can actually be found locally

A modest, well-planned site built on these principles will outperform an elaborate one that ignores them — and it will pay for itself in booked jobs, usually within months.

## The Right Way to Think About the Investment

Instead of asking "what's the cheapest website I can get," ask:

1. What is one additional job per month worth to me, in revenue?
2. What would this site need to do to produce that — and can the cheap option do it?
3. Over three years, which option actually costs less once results are counted?

A $3,000–$6,000 website that reliably produces jobs is not an expense next to a $500 website that produces nothing. One is marketing; the other is decoration.

And if your budget genuinely is tight right now, the answer isn't a bad full website — it's a small, excellent one: a fast homepage, three to five strong service pages, and flawless contact options. Grow it as revenue allows. Small and sharp beats big and broken every time.

## Quick Checklist

- [ ] Website cost evaluated against job value and leads produced, not just build price
- [ ] Site loads in under three seconds on a phone connection
- [ ] Phone number tappable and visible on every page
- [ ] One dedicated page per service you want calls for
- [ ] Real photos of your work and team, not stock images
- [ ] Reviews and credentials visible where decisions happen
- [ ] Short, working contact forms tested regularly
- [ ] You own your domain, content, and admin access
- [ ] Site structure built to rank locally (titles, headings, local content)
- [ ] Maintenance responsibility and costs understood up front
- [ ] If budget is tight: small and excellent, not big and broken
