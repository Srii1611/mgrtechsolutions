Most local service business websites fail at their one job: turning a visitor into a phone call. They're either digital business cards with nothing on them, or overbuilt messes stuffed with features nobody uses. The truth is that a high-performing contractor website needs a surprisingly short list of elements — done well. Here's the complete checklist, what to skip, and why each piece earns its place.

## First, Understand Who's Visiting and Why

Your visitors fall into three buckets:

1. **Emergency visitors** — pipe burst, AC dead, locked out. They're on a phone, stressed, and will call the first credible business that makes it easy.
2. **Researching visitors** — planning a project, comparing contractors, checking prices. They'll visit three to five sites before calling anyone.
3. **Verifying visitors** — got your name from a friend or saw your truck, now checking whether you're legit.

Every element below serves at least one of these three. Anything that serves none of them is decoration.

## The Non-Negotiables

### 1. A Tap-to-Call Phone Number, Everywhere

Your phone number should be visible at the top of every page and tappable on mobile. This alone separates lead-generating sites from brochures. Many contractors find that making the number prominent is the single highest-impact change they ever make — because most of their traffic is on phones, and calling is the goal.

### 2. A Clear Statement of What You Do and Where

Within three seconds, a visitor should know: what service you provide, what area you serve, and how to contact you. "Quality solutions for your home" says nothing. "Water heater repair and replacement in [County] — call or text" says everything.

### 3. Individual Pages for Each Service

One page per core service — not a bullet list on a single page. Each page should include:

- What the service covers and common problems it solves
- Rough pricing guidance or "what affects the cost" (honesty builds trust)
- Photos of your actual work
- A call to action: call, text, or request an estimate

These pages are how you rank in Google for specific searches, and they answer the researcher's questions before they have to ask.

### 4. Service Area Pages

If you serve multiple towns, your homepage can't rank for all of them. A page for each priority town — with genuine local detail, not a copy-paste template — can capture searches in communities where you have no physical address.

### 5. Real Photos of Real Work

Stock photos of smiling models holding wrenches actively hurt you. Visitors can smell them, and for service businesses, *seeing* the work is the trust trigger. Include:

- Before-and-after shots
- Your crew, your trucks, you
- Finished projects large and small

A phone camera and five minutes per job gives you a gallery no competitor can copy.

### 6. Reviews Displayed on the Site

Don't make visitors leave your site to find proof. Embed or display your best Google reviews — with the customer's first name and town where possible. A "What Customers Say" section near your contact form catches wavering visitors at the decision moment.

### 7. A Simple Contact Form

Keep it short: name, phone, service needed, brief description. Every extra field costs you submissions. A photo-upload option is a nice touch — homeowners love texting a picture of the problem instead of describing it. And make sure form submissions reach you reliably (test it monthly — broken forms silently kill leads).

### 8. Trust Signals: License, Insurance, Guarantees

Display your license number, insurance status, and any certifications or guarantees. For homeowners letting a stranger into their house, these aren't formalities — they're decision factors. Put them in the footer of every page and repeat them on your about page.

## The Strong Extras (Add These Once the Basics Are Solid)

- **An About page with your story and photo.** People hire people. A paragraph about why you started the business and who you are builds more trust than any slogan.
- **Pricing guidance content.** Pages or posts answering "How much does X cost?" attract ready-to-buy searchers and filter out ones who can't afford you. Both outcomes save you time.
- **FAQ section.** Answer the questions your office hears daily: scheduling, payment, warranties, service area, emergency availability.
- **Online scheduling or estimate request.** Especially valuable for non-emergency work — some customers will never call but will happily book online at 10 PM.
- **Financing information.** For high-ticket trades, mentioning financing options upfront removes a major hesitation.

## The Technical Foundations

These are invisible to visitors but decide whether they ever find you:

- **Mobile-first design.** Most of your traffic is phones. Test your entire site on an actual phone, not a desktop simulator.
- **Fast loading.** Compress every image — oversized photos are the top cause of slow contractor sites. Aim for pages that load in a few seconds on a cell connection.
- **HTTPS security.** The padlock in the browser bar. Without it, browsers warn visitors away.
- **Consistent business info.** Your name, address, and phone number must match your Google Business Profile exactly — it's a local ranking factor.
- **Clear page titles.** Each page's title should say what it is and where: "Drain Cleaning in [Town] | [Business Name]."

## What to Skip (Save Your Money)

- **Sliders and auto-playing carousels.** Almost nobody clicks past the first slide, and they slow your site.
- **Chatbots that can't answer anything.** If it can't book a job or answer real questions, it's an annoyance.
- **Fancy animations and video backgrounds.** Pretty in a demo, slow on a phone, worthless for conversions.
- **A blog nobody updates.** An empty blog with one post from three years ago looks worse than no blog. Either commit to one helpful post a month or skip it.
- **Social media feeds embedded on the homepage.** They distract from the call to action and often display outdated content.

## The 5-Second Self-Test

Hand your phone to a friend who's never seen your site. Give them five seconds on the homepage, then ask:

1. What does this business do?
2. Where do they work?
3. How would you contact them?

If they can't answer all three instantly, you know exactly what to fix. Then ask them to find proof the business is trustworthy. If it takes more than a few seconds, you know the next fix.

## Quick Checklist

- [ ] Tap-to-call phone number at the top of every page
- [ ] Homepage states what you do and where within three seconds
- [ ] One dedicated page per core service, each with photos and a call to action
- [ ] Service area pages for priority towns
- [ ] Real job photos — no stock imagery
- [ ] Google reviews displayed on the site
- [ ] Short contact form (4 fields or fewer), tested monthly
- [ ] License, insurance, and guarantee info in the footer
- [ ] Site loads fast and reads easily on a phone
- [ ] HTTPS enabled and business info matching Google exactly
- [ ] About page with your photo and story
- [ ] Passed the 5-second test with someone outside your business
