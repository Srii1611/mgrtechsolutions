General contracting is a relationship business dressed up as a search business. A homeowner planning a $75,000 addition or a whole-home remodel doesn't pick from the top of a map listing the way someone with a burst pipe does — they research for months, compare portfolios, and look for reasons to trust you with their house and their savings. This guide is about marketing that wins those bigger, better projects instead of competing for every small job in town.

## Understand How Big Projects Are Won

High-value clients move through a slower, more deliberate process:

1. **Dreaming:** saving photos, browsing remodels, imagining the space
2. **Feasibility research:** "what does a home addition cost," "how long does a kitchen remodel take"
3. **Vetting:** reading reviews, studying portfolios, checking licenses, asking friends
4. **Shortlisting:** two or three contractors invited to bid
5. **Decision:** rarely the lowest price — usually the contractor they trust most to communicate and finish

Your marketing should meet them in stages 1 through 3, so that by the shortlist, you're already the favorite. Competing only at the bidding stage means competing mostly on price — a race nobody wins.

## Your Portfolio Is Your Pitch

For big projects, nothing outsells documented work. Treat every job as a marketing asset:

- **Professional photos of your best projects.** For showcase jobs, hiring a real estate photographer for a few hundred dollars is some of the best marketing money you'll spend. Phones are fine for progress shots; finished work deserves better.
- **Before-and-after sets.** The cramped 1970s kitchen beside the open-concept result tells the whole story in two images.
- **Case studies, not just galleries.** For your best projects, write a short story: what the homeowner wanted, what challenges came up (surprise rot, a load-bearing wall, permit hurdles), how you solved them, and how it finished. Problems handled well are more persuasive than flawless photos — every big project has problems, and clients know it.
- **Organize by project type** so a visitor planning a bathroom sees bathrooms, and someone planning an addition sees additions.

## Content That Attracts Bigger-Project Clients

The questions big spenders search are different from small-job searches. Answer them better than anyone in your market:

- **Cost and budget content:** "What does a kitchen remodel cost in [your area]?" Give real ranges and explain what drives the number — size, finish level, structural changes, age of the house. Contractors who talk honestly about money attract clients with realistic budgets and repel the ones who'd waste your estimating time.
- **Process content:** "What actually happens during a whole-home renovation" — timelines, living arrangements, decision points, how change orders work. This content does double duty: it ranks in search and it pre-educates clients so your projects run smoother.
- **Planning content:** "Do you need an architect before calling a contractor?" "How to plan a home addition." You become the guide at the very start of the journey.
- **Local-specific content:** permitting timelines in your city, common issues in local housing stock (older wiring, slab foundations, historic districts). Local detail is what separates you from every generic national article.

A steady cadence of one or two substantial pieces a month builds a library that keeps working for years.

## Local SEO for the Long Game

Big projects still start with local searches: "general contractor [town]," "home addition contractor near me," "design build [city]."

- **Google Business Profile:** Category "General contractor," services listed (additions, remodels, new builds as applicable), and a constant flow of project photos. Post completed-project highlights monthly.
- **Reviews that speak to big projects:** Ask clients to mention the type of project and what it was like to live through it — communication, cleanliness, schedule, how surprises were handled. "They finished our addition on time and we could actually live in the house during it" is worth more than a generic five stars to your next $100,000 client.
- **Location pages** for the towns you want to work in, featuring projects you actually completed there.
- **Citations:** Houzz, Angi, the local home builders association, your chamber of commerce — with your name, address, and phone identical everywhere.
- **Awards and memberships:** NAHB membership, local "best of" awards, manufacturer certifications. Display them — they matter to careful researchers.

## Referrals: Still the Kingmaker

Most six-figure projects come from people, not search engines. Systematize it:

- **Architects and designers** are your highest-value referral source. Deliver their vision well, communicate proactively, and they'll bring you into projects before the client ever searches.
- **Real estate agents** know who just bought a fixer and who's renovating to sell.
- **Past clients:** Check in at six months and a year after completion. Not a sales call — "how's everything holding up?" Past clients refer when they're reminded you exist, and a maintenance touch-up visit keeps the relationship warm.
- **Trades and suppliers:** Plumbers, electricians, and lumberyards hear about projects early.
- **Make referring easy:** after a successful project, say it plainly — "Most of our work comes from referrals; if friends of yours are planning something, we'd be grateful for the introduction." Then thank every referral source, every time.

## Qualify Harder, Market to Fewer

Winning bigger projects also means spending less time on bad-fit leads. Your website can do that filtering:

- **State your project minimums or typical ranges.** "Our remodeling projects typically start at $40,000" feels bold, but it saves everyone time and quietly signals the level you operate at.
- **Show your process.** A page that explains your consultation, design, and estimating steps attracts clients who want a professional process — and deters those looking for three quick bids.
- **Use a thoughtful inquiry form** asking about project type, timeline, and budget range. Serious clients complete it; tire-kickers don't.

## Present Like the Price You Charge

Bigger projects are judged on the whole experience of dealing with you:

- Fast, organized responses to inquiries
- Branded, detailed proposals that explain scope clearly
- Photos and bios of your team — clients are inviting these people into their homes for months
- A clear communication plan: weekly updates, a point of contact, how changes are handled

Homeowners regularly pay a premium for the contractor who seems like they'll answer the phone in month three of the project. Your marketing should make that credibility obvious before the first meeting.
