You've probably gotten the calls. "We can get you to #1 on Google!" "Guaranteed leads in 30 days!" Some agency pitches sound great until you're three months into a contract with nothing to show but a lighter bank account. Choosing a marketing partner is one of the most consequential decisions you'll make for your business growth — and it's surprisingly easy to get wrong. This guide walks you through exactly what to look for, what to ask, and which red flags should send you running.

## Why This Decision Matters More Than You Think

A good marketing partner becomes an extension of your business. They learn your customers, your busy seasons, your margins, and they build systems that bring in work month after month. A bad one drains your budget, locks you into contracts, and sometimes leaves your online presence worse off than when they found it — spammy links pointed at your website, a Google Business Profile full of fake reviews, or ad accounts you don't even own.

The frustrating part: from the outside, good and bad agencies often look identical. Both have slick websites, confident salespeople, and impressive-sounding case studies. The difference shows up in the details, and the details are what we're going to cover.

## Step One: Get Clear on What You Actually Need

Before you talk to a single agency, spend an hour answering these questions:

- **What's the real problem?** "I need marketing" is too vague. Is it that your phone isn't ringing? That you're getting calls but not the right jobs? That competitors keep beating you on Google Maps? Each problem has a different fix and a different type of agency best suited to it.
- **What's a new customer worth to you?** If an average job is worth $400 and your close rate on calls is 50%, you can quickly work out what a lead is worth. This number keeps you honest when agencies quote prices.
- **What can you realistically invest — and for how long?** Most local SEO and content work takes 4–6 months before results compound. Paid ads work faster but stop the moment you stop paying. If you can only commit to 30 days of budget, that changes your options.
- **What can you handle in-house?** Maybe your office manager can post to Facebook, but nobody has time for the website. Knowing what you'll keep internally prevents paying twice for the same work.

Walking into agency conversations with these answers changes the dynamic completely. Instead of being sold to, you're interviewing them.

## The Types of Agencies (and What Each Is Actually Good At)

Not all agencies do the same things, and the label "digital marketing" covers a lot of ground:

| Type | Best For | Watch Out For |
|------|----------|---------------|
| Full-service agency | Businesses wanting one team for everything | Jack-of-all-trades work; you may pay for services you don't need |
| Local SEO specialist | Ranking in Google Maps and local search | Slower results; requires patience and consistent budget |
| PPC/paid ads agency | Fast lead flow, seasonal pushes | Leads stop when spend stops; needs a decent monthly budget to work |
| Web design shop | Rebuilding an outdated or broken website | Often weak on ongoing marketing after launch |
| Freelance consultant | Smaller budgets, one specific project | Capacity limits; what happens if they're sick or quit? |

For most local service businesses, the winning combination is a strong local SEO foundation plus a fast, conversion-focused website — with paid ads layered in when budget allows.

## Questions That Separate Real Agencies From Smooth Talkers

Bring this list to every sales call. The quality of the answers tells you almost everything.

**1. "Who specifically will work on my account?"**
The polished person on the sales call rarely does the work. Ask to meet the actual strategist or account manager. If they dodge, that's an answer.

**2. "Can you show me results for a business like mine?"**
Not a logo wall — an actual example. Ask what the situation was, what they did, how long it took, and what changed. A home services agency should be able to talk fluently about seasonality, service areas, and emergency vs. planned jobs.

**3. "What will you do in the first 90 days?"**
Good agencies have a clear onboarding process: audit, tracking setup, quick wins, then longer-term work. Vague answers like "we'll optimize your presence" mean there's no plan.

**4. "Do I own my website, ad accounts, and data?"**
The answer must be yes, in writing. Your Google Ads account, your analytics, your website files, your domain — all in accounts you control, with the agency granted access. Businesses have lost years of advertising history because everything lived in the agency's accounts.

**5. "How do you report, and what do the numbers mean?"**
Ask to see a sample report. It should show leads, calls, and cost per lead — not just impressions, clicks, and other vanity metrics. Ask them: "If the report shows something isn't working, what happens next?"

**6. "What do you need from me to be successful?"**
Honest agencies will tell you they need things: photos, review responses, quick approvals, access to call data. Agencies that say "we handle everything, you do nothing" usually deliver nothing.

**7. "What are the contract terms?"**
Month-to-month or a 90-day initial term is reasonable. Twelve-month lock-ins with big cancellation penalties benefit the agency, not you.

## Red Flags Worth Walking Away From

- **Guaranteed rankings.** Nobody controls Google. Anyone promising "#1 in 30 days" is either lying or planning tactics that could get your site penalized.
- **Secret sauce.** If they can't explain their methods in plain English, either the methods are shady or they don't respect you enough to include you.
- **Pricing far below market.** Quality local marketing takes real hours. Rock-bottom prices usually mean offshore templated work, your account managed by software, or an upsell ambush later.
- **They won't talk about your numbers.** An agency uninterested in your average job value and close rate can't possibly know if their work is profitable for you.
- **Pressure to sign today.** Legitimate agencies have capacity limits but don't manufacture urgency.
- **No verifiable reviews or references.** Ask for two or three current clients you can call. Actually call them.

## How to Evaluate the First 90 Days

Hiring the agency isn't the finish line — it's the start of a working relationship you'll need to manage. Set expectations up front:

- **Weeks 1–2:** Access granted, tracking installed, baseline numbers recorded. If you can't measure it, you can't judge it.
- **Weeks 3–6:** Foundational work delivered — website fixes, Google Business Profile optimization, citation cleanup, ad campaign builds. You should see real activity, not just "strategy sessions."
- **Weeks 7–12:** Early leading indicators. Rankings may not have jumped yet, but you should see movement: better map visibility, more calls tracked, faster site, new content live.

Schedule a recurring monthly call, keep it to 30 minutes, and always ask two questions: "What did you do?" and "What did it produce?" If six months pass with activity but no lead growth, it's fair to reassess.

## The Mindset That Gets Results

The businesses that get the most from their marketing partners treat it as a partnership, not a vending machine. Share your slow seasons so campaigns can ramp up early. Tell them which jobs are most profitable so ads chase the right work. Respond to the reviews they help you earn. Answer your phone — the best campaign in the world can't help if calls go to voicemail.

A good agency will make you money. A great one will also make you smarter about your own business. Take the time to choose well.

## Quick Checklist

- [ ] Define the specific problem you need solved before talking to any agency
- [ ] Calculate what one new customer is worth to your business
- [ ] Set a realistic budget and timeline (4–6 months minimum for SEO)
- [ ] Ask who will actually work on your account, not just who sells
- [ ] Request a real case study from a business similar to yours
- [ ] Get a written first-90-days plan
- [ ] Confirm in writing that you own your website, domain, and ad accounts
- [ ] Review a sample report and make sure it tracks leads, not vanity metrics
- [ ] Call two or three current client references
- [ ] Avoid guaranteed rankings, long lock-in contracts, and too-cheap pricing
- [ ] Schedule monthly check-ins and track leads from day one
