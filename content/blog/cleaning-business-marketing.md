Running a cleaning company means competing in one of the most crowded local markets there is. Whether you clean homes, offices, or both, you're up against franchise brands with big budgets, gig apps with slick technology, and a dozen other local crews fighting for the same customers. The good news: most of your competitors are doing marketing badly or not at all. This playbook covers the specific moves that fill a cleaning schedule — from showing up when locals search to winning the recurring contracts that make your revenue predictable.

## First, Decide What You're Really Selling

Before spending a dollar on marketing, get clear on your most profitable work, because "cleaning" is too broad to market well:

- **Recurring residential** (weekly/biweekly) — lower ticket, but predictable revenue and the backbone of most house cleaning businesses
- **One-time deep cleans and move-in/move-out** — higher ticket, one-off, great for filling gaps
- **Commercial contracts** (offices, medical, retail) — biggest revenue per client, longest sales cycle, most relationship-driven
- **Specialty work** (post-construction, vacation rentals, carpet) — less competition, premium pricing

Your marketing mix should match. Recurring residential is won on Google Maps and reviews. Commercial contracts are won through local visibility plus direct outreach and trust-building. Trying to market everything to everyone at once spreads a small budget too thin.

## Win the Map: Local SEO for Cleaning Companies

When someone searches "house cleaning near me" or "office cleaning [city]," the businesses in the Google Map Pack get the overwhelming majority of calls. Getting there is straightforward, if not instant.

### Nail Your Google Business Profile

Your Google Business Profile (GBP) is likely your single highest-return marketing asset — and it's free.

- **Choose categories carefully.** Primary category: "House cleaning service" or "Janitorial service" depending on your focus. Add secondary categories (carpet cleaning, pressure washing, window cleaning) only if you genuinely offer them.
- **Define your service area honestly.** List the cities and neighborhoods you actually serve. Overreaching dilutes your relevance.
- **Write a real business description.** Mention your core services, your differentiators (insured, background-checked staff, eco-friendly products), and the areas you serve — in natural language.
- **Post photos monthly.** Before-and-after shots, your team in uniform, your branded vehicle. Real photos outperform stock images and signal an active business.
- **Use the services section.** List each service with a short description. These help Google match you to specific searches like "move out cleaning."

### Build Reviews Like It's Part of the Job (Because It Is)

For cleaning businesses, reviews are the whole game. Customers are letting strangers into their homes or offices — trust is everything, and reviews are how strangers decide to trust you.

- Ask at the moment of maximum happiness: right after a walkthrough, after the first clean of a new recurring client, after saving a move-out deposit.
- Make it one tap: text or email a direct link to your Google review form.
- Respond to every review, good and bad. A calm, professional reply to a complaint often impresses prospects more than the complaint hurts.
- Aim for steady velocity — a few new reviews every week beats a burst of 30 followed by silence.
- Never buy reviews or offer incentives for them. Google removes them, and the risk to your profile isn't worth it.

### Get Your Local Citations Consistent

Your business name, address, and phone number (NAP) should be identical everywhere: your website, GBP, Yelp, Angi, Nextdoor, Facebook, your local chamber of commerce, and industry directories. Inconsistencies — an old phone number, a shortened street address — confuse Google and quietly suppress your rankings. Audit the top 20 listings once a year and fix anything outdated.

## Your Website: A Booking Machine, Not a Brochure

Most cleaning company websites fail at one job: making it effortless to request a quote. Yours should be built around that single action.

- **A real quote form above the fold.** Ask for home size, service type, and frequency — enough to price roughly — not just "name and email."
- **Transparent pricing guidance.** You don't need exact prices, but ranges ("most 3-bedroom homes run $X–$Y") dramatically increase form completions by removing the fear of a surprise.
- **Trust signals everywhere.** Insurance and bonding, background checks, satisfaction guarantee, how long you've been in business, team photos with real faces.
- **A page per service and per town.** A dedicated page for "deep cleaning in [suburb]" gives Google something specific to rank and gives searchers exactly what they typed.
- **Fast and mobile-first.** Most cleaning searches happen on a phone. If your site takes five seconds to load or the phone number isn't tap-to-call, you're losing jobs daily.
- **Online booking if you can manage it.** Many cleaning businesses find that offering instant booking for standard cleans lifts conversion noticeably — just make sure your scheduling capacity is real.

## Paid Ads: When and How to Use Them

Organic rankings take months. If you need the phone ringing sooner:

- **Google Local Services Ads (LSAs)** are often the best first paid channel for cleaning. You show at the very top with a "Google Guaranteed" badge and pay per lead, not per click. Getting verified (background checks, insurance documentation) takes a couple of weeks, so start early.
- **Google Search Ads** work well for high-intent terms like "move out cleaning [city]" but can burn money fast on broad terms. Start with a tight radius and exact services you want more of.
- **Facebook/Instagram ads** are better for promoting one-time offers (spring deep cleans, holiday prep) to homeowners in specific neighborhoods than for catching people ready to buy today.
- **Nextdoor** is an underrated channel for residential cleaning — neighbors actively ask for cleaner recommendations there, and a well-run business page plus occasional posts can generate steady referrals.

Set a monthly cap, track cost per booked job (not per click), and be honest: if you're paying $45 per lead and closing one in three, that's $135 per customer — fine for a recurring client worth thousands a year, painful for a one-time $150 clean.

## Winning Commercial Contracts

Commercial work follows different rules. Decision-makers — office managers, property managers, facility directors — don't impulse-buy from an ad. To win these:

- **Build a one-page capability statement** listing your services, insurance coverage, industries served, and references. This is your commercial calling card.
- **Optimize a dedicated commercial page** on your website targeting "office cleaning," "janitorial services," and "commercial cleaning" in your city.
- **Ask for Google reviews from commercial clients too**, and tag the industry in your reply ("glad the medical office team is happy...").
- **Do direct outreach.** Identify 20 target buildings or businesses, find the decision-maker, and follow up persistently but professionally. A short walkthrough offer works better than a hard sell.
- **Show up locally.** Chamber events, BNI chapters, and property management associations are where commercial relationships actually start.
- **Respond to RFPs fast.** When a request for proposal comes in, a polished, prompt, itemized bid beats a cheaper but sloppy one surprisingly often.

## The Referral Engine That Runs Itself

Your existing clients are your cheapest marketing channel:

- Offer a referral credit (e.g., $25–$50 off a future clean) for every referred client who books. Announce it quarterly.
- Leave-behind cards or fridge magnets keep you top of mind in the home.
- Partner with adjacent businesses — realtors (move-in/out cleans), property managers, carpet cleaners, organizers — for two-way referrals.
- Ask happy recurring clients to mention you in neighborhood Facebook groups when someone asks. A genuine neighbor recommendation is worth more than any ad.
