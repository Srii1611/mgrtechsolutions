You've built the business alone — every estimate, every job, every invoice — and now there's more work than one pair of hands can handle. Hiring your first employee is the biggest leap a solo owner ever takes. Do it right and you buy back your time and unlock growth. Do it wrong and you've added cost, stress, and legal risk. This playbook walks you through the decision, the numbers, and the process step by step.

## Step 1: Make Sure You're Actually Ready

Being busy isn't the same as being ready. Before you hire, check these four conditions:

- **The overflow is consistent.** You've been turning away work or pushing jobs out for at least two to three months — not just one unusually good month.
- **The work justifies the hours.** Track what you'd hand off. If you can't fill roughly 30+ hours a week of billable or clearly productive work, you're not ready for full-time (consider part-time or a subcontractor first).
- **The cash flow can cover it.** You should be able to pay wages for at least three months even if revenue dips — the first months rarely pay for themselves.
- **You know what the job actually is.** "Help me out" is not a job description. If you can't write down the top five responsibilities, you're hiring too early.

An honest gut check: many owners find the real trigger isn't workload — it's that admin, scheduling, and callbacks are eating the hours they should spend on billable work. Sometimes the right first hire is office help, not field help.

## Step 2: Run the Real Numbers

The wage is only the beginning. A common rule of thumb many owners use: an employee costs roughly 1.25–1.4 times their hourly wage once you add everything. Here's what goes into the multiplier:

| Cost | Rough Estimate |
|---|---|
| Wages | The number you negotiate |
| Payroll taxes (your share) | ~7.65% of wages, plus state taxes |
| Workers' compensation insurance | Varies widely by trade and state — often significant for field work |
| Tools, vehicle, fuel, uniforms | Real money, often forgotten |
| Training time (your lost billable hours) | Frequently the largest hidden cost |
| Software, phone, admin | Small but adds up |

**Example math:** You pay $20/hour. True cost lands around $26–$28/hour. For the hire to pay off, that person's hours need to free you to bill more than that — or directly produce billable work above that rate. If your billing rate is $85/hour and the hire lets you bill 20 more hours a month, that's $1,700 against roughly $4,500–$4,900 in monthly cost. The math only works when the hire takes billable work directly or meaningfully increases your own capacity. Run your numbers honestly before posting the job.

## Step 3: Handle the Legal Setup

This part isn't optional, and it isn't hard — just unfamiliar. Check your state and local requirements, but nearly every U.S. owner needs to:

1. **Get an EIN** (free from the IRS) if you don't already have one.
2. **Register with your state** for payroll tax withholding and unemployment insurance.
3. **Set up payroll.** Use a payroll service — the fees are modest and they handle tax filings, direct deposit, and year-end forms. Doing payroll manually is how owners end up in tax trouble.
4. **Buy workers' compensation insurance.** Required in nearly every state for employees, and the trades are exactly where injuries happen. Skip this and one accident can end your business.
5. **Verify work eligibility** (Form I-9) and collect a W-4 at hire.
6. **Check classification rules.** The line between "employee" and "independent contractor" is strictly enforced. If you control their schedule, tools, and methods, they're an employee — misclassification penalties are severe.
7. **Report new hires** to your state (required, usually within 20 days).

When in doubt, spend an hour with a local accountant or small-business attorney. It's the cheapest insurance you'll ever buy.

## Step 4: Write a Job Description That Filters For You

A good job post does half the hiring work. Include:

- **The real day-to-day:** tasks, physical demands, schedule, driving requirements
- **What you provide vs. what they bring:** tools, vehicle, licenses
- **Pay range:** posting it saves everyone time and widens your applicant pool
- **The deal-breakers up front:** valid driver's license, weekend availability, background check — whatever applies
- **What makes your business worth joining:** steady hours, growth potential, low-drama work environment. Good candidates have options.

### Where to Look

- Referrals from your network — suppliers, other trades, customers (often the best source)
- Local Facebook groups and community boards
- Indeed and similar job boards
- Trade schools and high school shop programs for entry-level hires
- "Help wanted" sign on your truck — old school, still works locally

## Step 5: Interview for Reliability, Not Just Skill

You can teach skills faster than you can teach character. For a first employee in a small business, reliability, honesty, and customer-friendliness outrank technical chops — this person will represent *your* name in customers' homes.

- **Ask behavior questions:** "Tell me about a time a job went wrong and what you did." "How do you handle a customer who's upset?"
- **Do a paid working interview** (where legal in your state) — a half-day on a real job tells you more than three conversations.
- **Check references actually.** Call past employers and ask one question: "Would you hire them again?" The pause before the answer tells you plenty.
- **Run background and driving checks** if they'll drive your vehicle or work in customers' homes — many insurance policies effectively require it.

## Step 6: Set Them Up to Succeed (The First 90 Days)

Most first-hire failures are management failures, not hiring failures. New employees quit or underperform when expectations live only in the owner's head.

**Week one:**
- Written offer letter covering pay, schedule, and responsibilities
- Signed handbook basics: show-up time, phone policy, vehicle rules, safety requirements
- Pair them with you on every job; narrate how you work and why

**Weeks two through six:**
- Gradually hand off tasks with clear standards ("this is what done looks like")
- Daily five-minute check-ins: what went well, what was unclear
- Correct small issues immediately and kindly — silence reads as approval

**Months two and three:**
- Let them run small jobs or tasks solo with you reachable
- Introduce them to customers as part of the business, not "the help"
- 30/60/90-day sit-downs: honest feedback both ways

### The Owner's Biggest Adjustment

Your job changes the day you hire. You'll spend time answering questions, checking work, and managing — time that used to be billable. Many owners report the first three to six months feel like going backward. That's normal. The payoff comes when the employee runs work without you — and you get to choose whether to grow, or simply stop working 70-hour weeks.

## Mistakes That Sink First Hires

- **Hiring fast out of desperation.** A bad hire costs more than a delayed one.
- **Skipping workers' comp or payroll taxes.** The short-term savings aren't worth the catastrophic downside.
- **Hiring a friend without treating it as employment.** Same standards, same paperwork, same accountability — or don't.
- **No written expectations.** Memory is not a management system.
- **Never letting go.** If you've trained, coached, and documented and it's still not working by 90 days, a clean, legal separation is kinder to everyone than slow-motion failure.

## Quick Checklist

- [ ] Confirmed 3+ months of consistent overflow work
- [ ] Verified cash reserves cover at least 3 months of total employment cost
- [ ] Calculated true cost (wages × 1.25–1.4) and confirmed the revenue math works
- [ ] EIN, state payroll registration, and payroll service set up
- [ ] Workers' compensation insurance bound before day one
- [ ] Written job description with pay range and deal-breakers posted
- [ ] Interviewed for reliability and character, not just skill
- [ ] References called; background/driving checks done where relevant
- [ ] Offer letter and written expectations signed before start date
- [ ] 90-day onboarding plan with 30/60/90 check-ins scheduled
- [ ] Honest self-check: ready to spend time managing, not just working
