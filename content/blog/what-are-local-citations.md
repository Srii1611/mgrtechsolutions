If you've spent any time reading about local SEO, you've run into the word "citations" — usually without a clear explanation of what they actually are or what you're supposed to do about them. This guide fixes that. By the end, you'll know exactly what a citation is, why search engines care, which ones matter, and how to build and maintain yours without wasting money.

## What Is a Local Citation, Exactly?

A local citation is any online mention of your business's core information — typically your **name, address, and phone number** (often shortened to "NAP"). Sometimes a citation also includes your website, hours, or a description.

Citations come in two flavors:

- **Structured citations** — listings on directories and platforms with standardized fields: your Google Business Profile, Yelp, Facebook, Bing Places, the Better Business Bureau, your local chamber of commerce directory, and industry-specific directories.
- **Unstructured citations** — mentions of your business information inside other content: a local news article, a blog post, a sponsor page, a community event listing.

A citation doesn't even need to link to your website to count. The mention itself is the signal.

## Why Search Engines Care About Citations

Google's job is to show searchers businesses that are real, legitimate, and accurately described. Citations help it do that in three ways:

### 1. Verification

When dozens of independent websites all say your business is "ABC Plumbing, 123 Main Street, 555-0142," Google gains confidence that you exist, you're located where you claim, and your phone number is real. Consistent citations are corroborating witnesses.

### 2. Prominence

A business listed across many reputable sites looks more established than one with no footprint. Citations contribute to the "prominence" factor that influences local rankings — especially in the map pack.

### 3. Discovery and direct traffic

Citations aren't just for algorithms. Real customers browse directories, and some platforms (particularly the big review sites) rank well in Google themselves — your listing there can capture customers who never reach your website.

## The Consistency Rule: NAP Accuracy Is Everything

Here's where most businesses quietly hurt themselves. Citations only help when your information matches everywhere. Common problems:

- **Old addresses or phone numbers** lingering on directories after a move or number change
- **Formatting variations** — "Suite 200" vs. "Ste 200" vs. "#200"
- **Business name drift** — "ABC Plumbing" on one site, "ABC Plumbing & Heating LLC" on another
- **Tracking numbers** used inconsistently instead of your main line

Individually these seem trivial. Collectively they dilute Google's confidence in your data, which can suppress your local rankings. Think of it this way: every mismatch is a small vote of uncertainty about your business.

**The fix:** pick one canonical version of your NAP — exactly as it appears on your Google Business Profile — and use that identical version everywhere.

## Which Citations Actually Matter?

You don't need to be listed on 300 directories. Citations follow a steep curve of diminishing returns, so focus in this order:

### Tier 1: The essentials (non-negotiable)

- Google Business Profile — the single most important listing you have
- Bing Places
- Apple Maps (via Apple Business Connect)
- Facebook
- Yelp
- Better Business Bureau (where applicable)

### Tier 2: Major data sources

A handful of large data aggregators and platforms feed business information to hundreds of smaller sites and apps. Getting your data right with the major ones — and on big platforms like Nextdoor and Angi where relevant — cascades accuracy across the ecosystem.

### Tier 3: Local and industry-specific

- Your local chamber of commerce and city business directories
- Trade association directories for your industry
- Local news site business listings

These carry extra weight because they're *relevant* — a plumber listed in a contractor directory and a local chamber site sends stronger topical and geographic signals than one listed on fifty generic directories.

**Rule of thumb:** 20–40 accurate, relevant citations beat 200 sloppy ones. Beyond that, your time is better spent on reviews and content.

## How to Audit Your Existing Citations

Before building anything new, find out what's already out there:

1. **Google your business name** and go several pages deep. Note every directory and site where you appear.
2. **Google your phone number** — this catches listings with wrong or missing business names.
3. **Google your address** in quotes — catches listings under old business names.
4. **Check for duplicates** on major platforms; duplicate listings confuse search engines and split your reviews.

Log everything in a spreadsheet: site, whether the info is correct, and what needs fixing. This audit is usually an eye-opener — most businesses find at least a few wrong phone numbers or zombie listings from years ago.

## How to Build Citations the Right Way

### Step 1: Fix your canonical NAP

Decide the exact, official version of your name, address, and phone. Update your website footer and Google Business Profile first — everything else should match these.

### Step 2: Claim and correct existing listings

Work through your audit spreadsheet. Claim each listing, correct the NAP, fill out profiles completely (hours, description, categories, photos), and request removal of duplicates. Start with Tier 1 and work down.

### Step 3: Build what's missing

Add listings on relevant Tier 2 and Tier 3 sites where you don't appear. Most are free; expect verification steps (postcard, phone, or email).

### Step 4: Keep it maintained

Citations aren't one-and-done. When anything changes — address, phone, hours, name — update everywhere, starting with the major platforms. A quick audit once or twice a year keeps drift from creeping back in.

## Common Citation Mistakes to Avoid

- **Buying bulk "1000 directory submissions" packages.** These spray your info across irrelevant, low-quality sites, often with errors. Worthless at best, messy at worst.
- **Using different phone numbers everywhere** for tracking. If you track calls, do it in ways that don't corrupt your core NAP.
- **Ignoring old listings** after a move or rebrand. Stale citations actively work against you.
- **Treating citations as a growth strategy.** They're a foundation, not a growth engine. Once yours are accurate and reasonably complete, additional directories move the needle very little — reviews, content, and your Google profile matter far more.

## Quick Checklist

- [ ] Make your website and Google Business Profile match it exactly
- [ ] Audit existing citations by searching your name, phone, and address
- [ ] Claim and correct your Tier 1 listings (Google, Bing, Apple, Facebook, Yelp)
- [ ] Remove or merge duplicate listings
- [ ] Fix NAP inconsistencies on all major directories
- [ ] Add relevant local and industry-specific directory listings
- [ ] Complete each profile fully: hours, description, categories, photos
- [ ] Skip bulk submission services — quality over quantity
- [ ] Re-audit your citations once or twice a year
