Opening a second or third location is a milestone — and an SEO puzzle. Suddenly you're not trying to rank in one town but several, and the approach that worked for your original location doesn't automatically multiply. Do it wrong and you end up with locations cannibalizing each other, thin duplicate pages Google ignores, and branches that are invisible in their own markets. Do it right and each location builds its own local presence while the whole brand grows stronger.

Here's how multi-location SEO actually works, in plain language, for service businesses with two to twenty locations.

## The Core Principle: Every Location Must Stand on Its Own

Google ranks *locations*, not companies, for local searches. When someone searches "HVAC repair in [town]," Google is looking for the best answer *in that town* — which means each of your branches needs to independently prove it belongs there:

- Its own page on your website with genuinely unique content
- Its own Google Business Profile tied to that page
- Its own reviews, earned by that branch
- Its own local citations and directory listings

Think of each location as its own small business that happens to share your brand and website. The branches that rank are the ones that look real and rooted in their community, not like a pin dropped on a map.

## Location Pages: Where Most Companies Get It Wrong

The most common multi-location mistake is the copy-paste location page: the same 300 words with the city name find-and-replaced. Google's seen this trick thousands of times and largely ignores it. A location page that ranks — and converts — contains things only true of that location:

**The essentials for every location page:**

- Location name and city in the page title and heading ("Plumbing Services in Beaverton")
- The branch's actual address (if it has one) and its own phone number
- Staffed-hours, services offered *at that branch*, and the specific towns it serves
- An embedded map and driving/parking notes
- Photos of that location, its trucks, and its crew — not stock imagery
- Reviews from that branch's customers, pulled in or quoted
- A short paragraph that's genuinely local: neighborhoods you serve, common local problems (hard water, older housing stock, soil conditions), community involvement

**A structure that works:** yoursite.com/locations/beaverton/ as the location hub, with the option of service-specific pages beneath it (yoursite.com/locations/beaverton/water-heater-repair/) once the location is established. Keep the URL structure clean and consistent across branches — it helps Google understand your footprint and helps you scale to location ten without chaos.

## Google Business Profiles: One Per Location, Managed Like Gold

Each physical location qualifies for its own Google Business Profile, and each one is that branch's ticket into the map pack. The rules that matter:

- **Every profile needs accurate, location-specific details:** its own address, phone number, hours, and a link to *its* location page — not your homepage.
- **Reviews accumulate per profile.** A five-star reputation at your flagship does nothing for your new branch. Each location needs its own review-generation habit from day one.
- **Choose categories carefully per location** if branches offer different services.
- **Service-area businesses have different rules.** If your "locations" are crews without staffed offices customers visit, you generally shouldn't create profiles for fake addresses — that's a suspension risk. Instead, use one profile with a service area, and lean harder on website location pages for geographic coverage.

This last point trips up a lot of expanding companies: a virtual office or a P.O. box is not a location in Google's eyes, and getting caught costs you all your profiles, not just the fake one.

## Reviews: Decentralize the Asking

Multi-location review strategy has one rule: **the review request must attach to the branch that did the work.** That means:

- Crews and office staff ask for reviews mentioning the location naturally ("our Beaverton team")
- Your review links point to each branch's own Google profile, not a single corporate one
- Someone owns review responses per location — responses that reference the local team feel authentic and reinforce the geography

New locations start at zero, which feels painful, but a concentrated 90-day review push at launch usually builds enough of a base to compete.

## Citations and Local Links, Per Location

Each branch needs its own consistent listings — name, address, phone — across the major directories (Yelp, BBB, Angi, Facebook, industry directories, your local chambers). Two disciplines matter here:

1. **Consistency.** If your Springfield branch is "ABC Plumbing — Springfield" in one directory and "ABC Plumbing LLC" in another, with a tracking number on a third, you're diluting that location's signals. Pick a canonical name and number per branch and use them everywhere.
2. **Local links.** Links from local sources — sponsorships, chamber memberships, local news, community event pages — are powerful exactly because they're geographic. A little league sponsorship in each branch's town does more for local rankings than a generic national directory ever will.

## Avoiding the Cannibalization Trap

When two of your locations serve overlapping areas, they can compete with each other in search results, splitting your visibility. Prevent it by:

- **Defining service areas per location page** and being explicit about which towns each branch covers
- **Not creating pages for towns where you have no real presence** — fifty near-duplicate "service area" pages with no substance is the copy-paste mistake at scale
- **Letting the nearest branch own each town**, and cross-linking between location pages where boundaries blur
- **Using one domain.** Resist any urge to build separate websites per location (abcmplumbingbeaverton.com, etc.). Multiple sites split your authority, multiply your maintenance, and almost always underperform one strong domain with a locations section.

## Scaling Without Losing Your Mind

The jump from two locations to eight breaks informal systems. Before you scale:

- **Template the location page** with mandatory unique-content sections, so new branches launch fast without the copy-paste trap
- **Assign review ownership per branch** — a named person responsible for asking and responding
- **Standardize naming and tracking** — one phone-number and listing convention applied everywhere
- **Measure per location.** Calls, form fills, and rankings tracked by branch tell you which locations need help. A branch invisible in search after six months usually has a specific gap — few reviews, thin page content, inconsistent citations — and the data will show you which.

## Quick Checklist

- [ ] Created a dedicated, unique-content page for each location on one domain
- [ ] Set up a separate Google Business Profile per physical location, linked to its own page
- [ ] Verified name, address, and phone consistency per branch across all directories
- [ ] Built a review request process that attaches reviews to the correct branch
- [ ] Added real photos, staff details, and local specifics to every location page
- [ ] Defined each branch's service area to prevent locations competing with each other
- [ ] Removed or avoided fake addresses, virtual offices, and duplicate service-area pages
- [ ] Set up per-location tracking for calls, forms, and rankings
- [ ] Documented the template so the next location launches faster than the last
