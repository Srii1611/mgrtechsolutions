You don't need a production company, a script, or a single piece of equipment beyond the phone in your pocket. Video is the most trust-building content a local service business can create — customers can see your work, hear your voice, and decide they like you before you ever answer their call. This guide shows you exactly what to film, how to film it, and where to put it, all in the spare minutes between jobs.

## Why Video Works So Well for Local Services

Hiring a contractor means letting a stranger into your home and trusting them with thousands of dollars. Text and photos help, but video closes the trust gap faster than anything else:

- **It proves you're real.** In industries plagued by fly-by-night operators, a business owner on camera saying "Here's what we did today" is instantly credible.
- **It demonstrates expertise effortlessly.** You don't have to claim you're knowledgeable — explaining a problem on video shows it.
- **It pre-sells your personality.** Customers hire people they like. Video lets them meet you before they call.
- **It gets favored by platforms.** Google Business Profile, Facebook, and Instagram all push video to more people than plain text posts.

Many contractors find that their simplest, least-polished videos outperform everything else they post — because they feel genuine.

## The Five Types of Videos That Actually Get Customers

You never need to wonder what to film. Rotate through these five:

### 1. The Job Walk-Through (30–60 seconds)

Film at the end of a job: "We're in [Town] today — this homeowner had [problem]. Here's what we found, and here's the finished fix." Walk the camera across the work while you talk. This is your bread-and-butter video: proof of work, local relevance, and expertise in one clip.

### 2. The Quick Tip (30–45 seconds)

Answer one question customers ask constantly: "Here's the first thing to check if your furnace won't start." "This is how you know a contractor's quote is too low." One tip per video. These get shared, and they position you as the helpful expert rather than a salesperson.

### 3. The Before-and-After Reveal (15–30 seconds)

Show the problem, show the result. Add one sentence of context. These are the most shareable videos you can make — dramatic transformations travel far on Facebook and Instagram.

### 4. The Meet-the-Team Clip (20–45 seconds)

Quick intros: "This is Mike, he's been with us four years, and he's the guy who'll probably answer your call." Customers let strangers into their homes — familiar faces lower that barrier.

### 5. The Customer Moment (with permission)

A happy customer saying one sentence on camera at the end of a job is worth more than any ad. Always ask permission, keep it casual, and never pressure. If they decline, the job walk-through is plenty.

## How to Film (The Entire Skill Set You Need)

Forget equipment lists. Your phone is enough if you follow six rules:

1. **Hold it horizontally** (or vertically for Reels/Shorts — just pick one per video and stay consistent).
2. **Face the light.** Stand so the sun or room light hits your face, not the camera. Backlit video looks terrible; this one fix changes everything.
3. **Get close for audio.** Phone microphones are fine within a few feet. Film narration in your truck cab or a quiet room if the job site is loud — you can lay it over footage later, or just film a quiet summary clip.
4. **Keep it steady.** Brace your elbows, or lean the phone against something. Fancy gimbals are unnecessary.
5. **Keep it short.** Under 60 seconds covers almost everything. If you need two minutes, make two videos.
6. **Clean the lens.** A pocket-wiped lens is the cheapest upgrade in video production.

**On appearing on camera:** you don't need to be polished. Flub a word? Keep going — it reads as human. Many business owners find their tenth video feels natural, and nobody remembers the first nine anyway.

## The 15-Minute Production System

Here's the workflow that makes video sustainable for a working business:

1. **Film during the job (2–3 min):** A clip of the problem, a clip mid-work, a clip of the result. Three short clips beat one long one.
2. **Film your summary in the truck (2 min):** "Here's what we did and why it matters."
3. **Trim on your phone (3 min):** Free built-in editors (iPhone Photos, Google Photos, or CapCut) trim clips. Cut the dead air at the start and end. Skip fancy transitions.
4. **Post with a plain-language caption (5 min):** "Removed a dead maple hanging over a garage in [Town] today. Here's the before and after." Tag nothing fancy; write like you talk.

Total: about 15 minutes per finished video, using time you already spend on the job.

## Where to Post (In Priority Order)

You don't need to be everywhere. This order maximizes return per minute:

1. **Google Business Profile** — videos here directly support your most important lead channel. Post job walk-throughs and tips.
2. **Facebook** — still where most local homeowners actually spend time. Before-and-afters and team clips shine here.
3. **Instagram Reels** — if you're already posting to Facebook, cross-posting Reels costs you one extra tap.
4. **YouTube (Shorts and longer)** — YouTube videos can rank in Google search for years. Upload everything here too; it's free shelf space with a long tail.
5. **Your website** — embed your best two or three videos on your homepage and gallery page. Video keeps visitors on your site longer, and longer visits convert better.

## The Repurposing Multiplier

One job can feed your content for a week:

- Three raw clips → one walk-through video
- The same clips → one before/after video
- A still frame → a photo post
- The tip you mentioned → a short text post next week
- The video embedded in a related blog post → better page engagement

Film once, publish five times.

## What Actually Matters (and What Doesn't)

**Matters:** showing your face, showing real work, posting consistently (one video a week beats five in one weekend), local mentions ("here in [Town]"), and decent light.

**Doesn't matter:** fancy equipment, scripted delivery, logos and intro animations, background music, or perfect hair. Homeowners hire authentic experts, not commercial actors.

## Getting Past the "I Hate Being on Camera" Wall

Nearly every business owner does at first. Three tricks that work:

- **Start with narration only.** Film the work and talk from behind the camera. No face required for your first ten videos.
- **Talk to one person.** Imagine explaining the job to a friendly customer, not "an audience."
- **Set a quota, not a quality bar.** Commit to one video a week for eight weeks, no deleting. Improvement is automatic; starting is the hard part.

- [ ] Weekly filming habit tied to job completion
- [ ] Videos posted to Google Business Profile first

- [ ] Captions written in plain, local language ("in [Town] today...")
- [ ] Best videos embedded on website homepage or gallery
- [ ] Everything cross-posted to YouTube for long-term search value
- [ ] One video per week for eight weeks — no deleting, no judging
