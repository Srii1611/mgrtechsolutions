Scroll through the local results for almost any trade and you'll see the same thing: profiles with three blurry photos from 2019, a stock image of a smiling stranger, and a logo as the cover photo. Meanwhile, the businesses winning the clicks have bright, real, recent photos of actual work and actual people. Photos on your Google Business Profile aren't decoration — they're one of the strongest trust signals a potential customer gets before ever visiting your website. This guide covers exactly what to post, how often, and how to take good photos with nothing but the phone already in your pocket.

## Why Photos Punch Above Their Weight

When someone finds your profile, they make a trust decision in seconds. Before they read a single review, they've glanced at your photos and silently answered: *Does this business look real? Professional? Active?*

Real photos answer all three at once:

- **They prove you exist.** A branded truck, a uniformed crew, a real storefront — these confirm you're a legitimate local operation, not a lead-selling shell company.
- **They pre-sell your quality.** A crisp before-and-after of a bathroom remodel does more convincing than three paragraphs of sales copy.
- **They signal activity.** Photos dated last week tell both customers and Google that your business is alive and working.
- **They drive measurable engagement.** Google has long reported that profiles with photos earn substantially more requests for directions and more clicks to websites than profiles without. Most businesses that commit to regular photo uploads notice the difference in their profile engagement within a couple of months.

## The Photo Types Every Local Business Needs

Think of your photo library as covering six categories. You don't need hundreds of photos — you need good coverage of each type.

### 1. Your Team

Real faces build trust faster than anything else. Customers are inviting you into their homes or handing you their property — they want to see who shows up.

- Group shot of the crew in uniform or branded gear
- Individual photos of techs or crew members on the job
- You, the owner — especially if you're the face of the business

Keep it natural. People at work, smiling mid-job, beats stiff lineup poses.

### 2. Your Vehicles

For service businesses, your truck or van is often the most recognizable thing you own. A clean, branded vehicle photo does double duty: it builds trust online, and customers start recognizing your vehicles around town — which reinforces everything else.

### 3. Completed Work

The bread and butter of your library:

- Finished projects: the new roof, the installed unit, the sparkling kitchen
- Detail shots that show craftsmanship — clean pipework, straight tile lines, tidy wiring
- Wide shots that show the whole result

### 4. Before-and-Afters

These are the highest-converting photos a local service business can post. Take the "before" shot the moment you arrive — make it a habit, like putting on your gloves. Then capture the "after" from the same angle. Same angle matters: it's what makes the transformation obvious.

### 5. Your Workspace or Storefront

If customers visit you, exterior and interior shots help them find you and feel comfortable walking in — especially the exterior, since it answers "what am I looking for?" If you're home-based or mobile-only, skip this and lean harder into team and work photos.

### 6. Work in Progress

Crews on ladders, equipment in use, a job mid-way. These photos communicate busyness and real activity better than polished finals alone.

## Which Photos to Use for Each Profile Slot

Your profile has a few specific photo slots, and they deserve deliberate choices:

- **Logo:** Your actual logo, on a clean background. Simple.
- **Cover photo:** This is your profile's first impression and often appears in search results. Choose your single best image — a sharp team-and-truck shot or a dramatic finished project. Test a couple of options; Google sometimes selects its own cover based on engagement, but a strong suggestion helps.
- **General photos:** Everything else, organized loosely by the categories above.

## How to Take Good Photos With Just a Phone

You don't need a photographer. You need a few habits:

- **Wipe the lens.** Half of all blurry job-site photos are just a dusty lens. It takes two seconds.
- **Use natural light.** Shoot outdoors or near windows when possible. If indoors, turn on every light in the room.
- **Hold steady and tap to focus** on the subject before shooting.
- **Shoot horizontal (landscape)** for most work shots — it fits profile displays better.
- **Take three, keep one.** Burst a few versions and pick the sharpest later.
- **Clean the scene.** Move the coffee cups, tools clutter, and trash out of frame. A tidy job site photographs as a professional one.
- **Step back.** The most common mistake is shooting too close. Capture the whole room, the whole roofline, the whole vehicle.

## File Details That Help

Before uploading:

- **Name files descriptively:** `kitchen-remodel-after-phoenix.jpg` instead of `IMG_8834.jpg`. It's a small relevance signal and keeps your library organized.
- **Use decent resolution:** modern phone cameras are more than enough. Avoid heavily compressed screenshots or images forwarded through messaging apps multiple times.
- **Skip heavy filters.** Natural, accurate color builds trust. Save the drama for Instagram.
- **Geotagging:** Photos taken on your phone at the job location often carry location data automatically. Don't go out of your way to strip it — it's a minor but harmless signal of real local activity.

## What NOT to Post

- **Stock photos.** Customers recognize them instantly, and they actively erode trust. Google's guidelines also expect photos to represent your actual business.
- **Other people's work.** Never use manufacturer photos or images from the internet to represent jobs you did.
- **Low-effort screenshots** of invoices, text messages, or software dashboards.
- **Anything with customer personal information** visible — house numbers are fine, but avoid visible documents, license plates where possible, and people's faces unless they've agreed.
- **Heavily watermarked images.** A small logo in the corner is fine; a giant watermark across the photo looks desperate.

One more courtesy worth building into your process: when photographing inside a customer's home or business, a quick "mind if we grab a photo of the finished work for our portfolio?" is almost always met with a yes — and it's the right thing to do.

## A Simple Upload Rhythm That Sticks

The businesses with great photo libraries didn't take them all in one weekend — they built the habit. Here's a rhythm that works:

- **On every job:** one before shot, one after shot. Non-negotiable, like cleaning up the site.
- **Weekly:** someone on the team uploads the best two or three photos from the week.
- **Monthly:** check your library against the six categories. Thin on team photos? Get a group shot at the next morning meeting. Nothing seasonal? Take one that fits the time of year.
- **Quarterly:** prune outdated photos — old branding, former employees, work that no longer represents your quality.

Assign this to a specific person. "Everyone should upload photos" reliably means nobody does.

## Quick Checklist

- [ ] Upload your logo and choose a strong cover photo
- [ ] Add at least ten real photos across all six categories
- [ ] Photograph your team, vehicles, and workspace
- [ ] Make before-and-after shots a standard part of every job
- [ ] Wipe your lens and use natural light for every shot
- [ ] Name files descriptively before uploading
- [ ] Remove all stock images and manufacturer photos
- [ ] Ask customers' permission before photographing their property
- [ ] Upload two or three fresh photos every week
- [ ] Review and prune your photo library quarterly
