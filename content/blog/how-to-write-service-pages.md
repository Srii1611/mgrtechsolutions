Your service pages are the hardest-working pages on your website. When someone searches "water heater repair near me" or "deck building [your town]," these are the pages that should show up — and once a visitor lands, they have seconds to convince that person to call. Most service businesses get this wrong with a single page listing everything they do. Here's how to write service pages that both rank in search and turn visitors into customers.

## Why One Page Per Service Matters

Search engines match pages to specific searches. A page titled "Services" with a bullet list of twenty offerings can't rank strongly for any of them — it's about everything, so it's about nothing. A dedicated page about drain cleaning, on a site clearly belonging to a plumber in your town, can.

There's a second benefit: visitors convert better when the page is about *their* problem. A homeowner with a clogged sewer line who lands on a page entirely about sewer line clogs feels understood. One who lands on a generic services list has to hunt for reassurance — and most won't bother.

Start with your five to ten most profitable or most requested services. One page each. You can add more over time.

## The Anatomy of a Service Page That Works

Every strong service page contains the same core elements, in roughly this order:

### 1. A clear headline that names the service and the place

"Water Heater Repair & Replacement in [Town]" beats "Welcome to Our Website" every time. The headline tells visitors — and search engines — they're in the right place.

### 2. An immediate path to contact

A tappable phone number and a short form or booking button near the top. Urgent visitors shouldn't have to read a word to reach you.

### 3. Symptoms and situations in the customer's words

Describe the problems this service solves the way customers describe them: "water pooling around the base of the heater," "no hot water by the second shower," "a popping or rumbling sound from the tank." This does two jobs — it reassures visitors you understand their exact situation, and it matches the phrases real people type into search.

### 4. What the service actually involves

Walk through your process in plain language: what you inspect, what a typical repair looks like, how long it takes, whether permits are involved. This sets expectations, filters out bad-fit inquiries, and demonstrates competence better than any claim of "quality service" ever could.

### 5. Honest pricing guidance

You don't need a price list, but give something: a range, a starting point, or a clear explanation of what affects the cost ("depends on the unit size, venting, and whether the shut-off valves need replacing"). Pages that address cost openly tend to convert better, because pricing anxiety is the biggest silent reason visitors leave without calling.

### 6. Proof you're trustworthy

Two or three reviews from customers who had *this* service done. A photo of your team doing this work. Your license, insurance, and any relevant certifications. A guarantee stated in plain words. Sprinkle these through the page, not buried at the bottom.

### 7. Answers to the questions people actually ask

Add a short FAQ section: "How long does a water heater last?" "Can you fix it the same day?" "Do you haul away the old unit?" You already answer these on the phone daily — write them down once and let the page do it forever. Question-and-answer content also aligns well with how people search.

### 8. A clear, specific call to action at the end

Close with a direct invitation: "Call [number] for same-day water heater service in [town], or book online — we'll confirm your window within the hour." One page, one primary action.

## Write for Stressed Humans, Not Search Engines

The old approach — repeating the keyword awkwardly in every paragraph — is dead, and it read terribly anyway. What works now is simply being the most helpful page on the topic in your area:

- **Write like you talk on a job site.** Plain sentences, no jargon without a quick explanation.
- **Keep paragraphs short.** Two to four sentences. Visitors scan; walls of text lose them.
- **Use headings that say something.** "What causes a sewer line to back up" is better than "More information."
- **Be honest about DIY limits.** "You can try a plunger first — but if the clog keeps returning, it's usually deeper in the line" builds trust and pre-qualifies your calls.

Length follows substance, not the other way around. Most solid service pages land somewhere between 600 and 1,200 words. If you can genuinely help someone in 600 words, stop there. Padding ranks worse than clarity.

## The Local Layer

Two things make a service page *locally* relevant rather than generic:

- **Mention the area naturally.** Your town and service area in the headline, the intro, and a closing line — plus local detail only a local would know: "many homes in [neighborhood] still have original galvanized lines," "hard water in this area shortens tank life."
- **Keep your business info consistent.** Your name, address, and phone number should appear on the page (a footer works) exactly as they appear on your business listings elsewhere.

If you serve multiple towns, build location-specific versions only where you can add genuine local detail — near-duplicate pages with swapped town names can do more harm than good.

## Photos: Proof, Not Decoration

Every service page should include at least one real photo of your crew doing that service — not a stock image. Customers have learned to spot stock photography instantly, and it quietly undermines everything the words claim. A slightly imperfect photo of your tech replacing a water heater is worth more than a polished stock image of a stranger holding a wrench.

Compress images before uploading so the page loads fast on phones, and give each image a descriptive file name and alt text ("tech-replacing-water-heater-[town].jpg") — it helps accessibility and search visibility.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **One page listing all services** — split them up
- **No phone number until the contact page** — put it everywhere
- **Copy identical to a competitor's or manufacturer boilerplate** — write your own, in your voice
- **Talking about yourself first** — lead with the customer's problem, not your company history
- **No pricing information at all** — give ranges or factors at minimum
- **Set it and forget it** — review your top pages twice a year; update prices, photos, and anything outdated

## Quick Checklist

- [ ] Separate page for each major service you want calls for
- [ ] Headline names the service and location
- [ ] Tap-to-call number and booking option visible at the top
- [ ] Symptoms described in the customer's own words
- [ ] Process explained step by step in plain language
- [ ] Pricing ranges or cost factors addressed honestly
- [ ] Service-specific reviews and real work photos included
- [ ] FAQ section answering real customer questions
- [ ] One clear call to action closing the page
- [ ] Local details and consistent business info throughout
- [ ] Images compressed with descriptive file names
- [ ] Top pages reviewed and refreshed twice a year
