If your business serves more than one town, you've probably run into this question: should your website have pages for each city you *serve*, or only for the places you actually have a *location*? The two approaches sound similar but behave very differently in Google — and picking the wrong one wastes months of effort. This guide explains the difference in plain terms, when each approach wins, the hybrid structure most growing businesses should use, and the mistakes that get sites ignored or penalized.

## The Definitions, Without the Jargon

**Location pages** represent physical places where customers can find you: your office, showroom, shop, or branch. "Our Denver location — 123 Main Street, open 8–5, here's the team you'll meet." Each location page corresponds to a real address, and usually to its own Google Business Profile.

**Service area pages** represent places you *travel to* but aren't based in. You're a plumber headquartered in Denver who also serves Aurora, Lakewood, and Arvada. Those towns get service area pages: "Plumbing services in Aurora — our crew is twenty minutes away, here's the work we do there."

The distinction matters because Google treats physical presence and service coverage differently. A real address can earn a spot in the Map Pack for that town. Coverage alone mostly competes in the organic results below the map.

## When Location Pages Are the Right Call

Location pages fit businesses where customers come to you, or where you genuinely operate staffed branches:

- Storefront businesses: showrooms, retail, clinics, restaurants
- Multi-branch service companies with real, staffed offices in each city
- Any business where each address has its own team, hours, or inventory

If that's you, the playbook is straightforward:

1. **One page per real location**, with its own address, local phone number, hours, photos of that actual site and team, and directions/parking details.
2. **A separate Google Business Profile for each location** — this is what unlocks Map Pack visibility in each city.
3. **Reviews earned per location**, so each branch builds its own reputation.
4. **Unique content per page.** If your two branches have the same page with the city swapped, you've missed the point — describe each team, each neighborhood, each location's character.

The critical word throughout is *real*. Some businesses are tempted to rent virtual offices or use employees' home addresses to fake "locations" in target cities. Google actively suspends profiles for this, and a suspended profile can take months to recover. Not worth it.

## When Service Area Pages Are the Right Call

Service area pages fit businesses that go to the customer:

- Plumbers, electricians, HVAC techs, roofers, cleaners, landscapers
- Mobile services of every kind
- Single-location businesses covering a metro area or region

Here, your one Google Business Profile is set up as a **service-area business**: your address is hidden (if customers don't visit you), and your service area is defined by the towns and zip codes you cover. Your website's service area pages then tell the story town by town.

The ground rules:

- **Only build pages for towns you genuinely serve and want work in.**
- **Every page needs unique, substantive content** — local job examples, town-specific considerations, real photos, testimonials from customers there.
- **One page per town**, not one page per service per town. A page per service per city across twenty towns is a 400-page doorway farm, and Google treats it like one.

## The Decision Framework

Run your situation through these questions:

| Question | If yes | If no |
|----------|--------|-------|
| Do customers visit you at the address? | Location page + GBP for it | Service area model |
| Do you have staffed offices in multiple cities? | Location page per office | Service area pages |
| Do you travel to customers across many towns? | Service area pages | — |
| Can you write genuinely unique content for each town? | Build pages for those towns | Fewer, stronger pages |
| Is the town's competition weak? | Higher priority target | Longer-term target |

A simple rule of thumb covers most cases: **a page exists because a real customer from that town would find it useful and accurate** — not because a keyword exists.

## The Hybrid Approach Most Growing Businesses Should Use

Many businesses evolve into a hybrid, and that's fine — as long as the structure stays clean:

- **Location pages** for your real, staffed locations (each with its own GBP)
- **Service area pages** for the surrounding towns each location covers
- **One "areas we serve" hub page** that links to everything, so visitors and Google can navigate the structure easily

Example: a two-branch HVAC company has a location page for each branch, plus service area pages for the six or eight suburbs each branch realistically covers — with each suburb page assigned to the branch that actually serves it.

Two cautions for hybrids:

- **Keep service area pages close to home.** Pages for towns 90 minutes away rarely rank and rarely convert; customers sense the distance.
- **Don't let the page count outrun your substance.** Ten excellent pages beat sixty thin ones — and thin pages can drag down your whole site's credibility with Google.

## Mistakes That Sink Both Approaches

- **Find-and-replace pages.** Identical text with the city name swapped is the classic doorway page. Google has discounted these for years; at scale, they can earn a manual penalty.
- **Fake addresses.** Virtual offices and P.O. boxes used to create phony "locations" violate Google's guidelines and risk profile suspension.
- **Keyword-stuffed URLs and headings.** "best-cheap-emergency-plumber-aurora-co" reads as spam to customers and algorithms alike. Clean and natural wins.
- **Orphan pages.** If nothing links to your city pages — no hub page, no menu, no internal links — Google may never take them seriously.
- **Set-and-forget.** Pages that never get new photos, reviews, or updates slowly fade. A light quarterly refresh keeps them earning.

## Measuring Whether Your Structure Works

Give any new page three to six months, then check:

- **Google Search Console:** impressions and positions for "service + town" queries
- **Calls and form fills** attributed to those pages
- **Map Pack presence** — realistically, expect location pages (with their GBP) to appear in maps, and service area pages to win in organic results

If a page ranks on page two after honest effort, it usually needs more substance — more local proof, more photos, a customer story — not more keywords. If it ranks but doesn't convert, the page's job changes: better offer, clearer phone number, faster load.

## Quick Checklist

- [ ] Decide per town: do customers visit you there (location page) or do you travel there (service area page)?
- [ ] Create one Google Business Profile per real staffed location only
- [ ] Hide your address and set service areas on your GBP if customers don't visit you
- [ ] Build one unique, substantive page per town — never find-and-replace
- [ ] Assign each service area page to the nearest real branch
- [ ] Create an "areas we serve" hub linking to every page
- [ ] Add real local proof to each page: jobs, photos, reviews from that town
- [ ] Skip towns you can't genuinely serve well
- [ ] Review each page quarterly and refresh with new local material
- [ ] Measure rankings and leads per page after 3–6 months
