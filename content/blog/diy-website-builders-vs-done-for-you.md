You know you need a website — or a better one. The question is whether you should spend your evenings dragging boxes around in a DIY builder or pay someone to handle the whole thing while you stay on the tools. Both routes can work, and both can waste your money if you pick the wrong one for your situation. This guide breaks down the real costs, the hidden trade-offs, and a simple way to decide.

## What "DIY Builder" and "Done-For-You" Actually Mean

Before comparing, let's be precise about what you're choosing between.

**DIY website builders** are platforms like Wix, Squarespace, Shopify, or the site builders bundled with GoDaddy and Google. You pick a template, swap in your photos and text, and publish. No coding, monthly fee typically between $15 and $40.

**Done-for-you services** cover a wide range: a freelancer who builds you a WordPress site, a local web agency, or a marketing company that builds *and* maintains your site as part of a monthly package. Prices range from a few hundred dollars for a basic freelancer job to several thousand for an agency build, often plus a monthly fee for hosting, updates, and support.

The key difference isn't the tool — it's whose time and expertise goes into the site.

## The Real Cost of DIY (It's Not Just the Monthly Fee)

DIY builders advertise prices that look like a rounding error next to an agency quote. But the sticker price is only part of it.

**Your time is the biggest line item.** A first-timer typically spends 20 to 60 hours getting a site from template to "actually ready to show customers." If your billable rate is $85 an hour, that's $1,700 to $5,100 of your time — and the result is usually a first draft, not a polished site.

**Then there's the ongoing time cost.** Every photo update, every new service page, every "why does the contact form look broken on my phone" moment lands on your desk. Many business owners find their DIY site quietly goes stale because updating it never feels urgent — until a customer mentions the site lists a service you stopped offering two years ago.

**And the quality ceiling.** Templates get you 80% of the way visually. The last 20% — the part that makes a site load fast, read clearly on a phone, and actually convince someone to call — usually requires experience you don't have time to build.

DIY isn't "cheap." It's "paid for in your hours instead of dollars."

## Where DIY Builders Genuinely Win

None of that means DIY is a bad choice. For the right situation, it's the smart one:

- **You're brand new and cash is tighter than time.** A clean, simple DIY site beats no site, and it beats going into debt for a fancy build before you have steady revenue.
- **Your needs are truly simple.** If you need five pages — home, about, services, photos, contact — and nothing fancy, a modern template handles that fine.
- **You (or someone on your team) actually enjoys this stuff.** Some owners genuinely like tinkering. If that's you, the time cost is more hobby than burden.
- **You want full control.** Need to change your holiday hours at 9pm? You just do it. No support ticket, no waiting.

A lot of successful local businesses started on a $20-a-month builder and upgraded later. There's no shame in that path — it's often the right one.

## Where Done-For-You Earns Its Money

A good done-for-you provider isn't just "making pages." You're paying for things that are hard to DIY:

**Strategy before pixels.** A decent pro asks who your best customers are, what they search for, and what makes them pick up the phone — then builds around those answers. Templates can't do that.

**Performance and mobile polish.** Most local searches happen on a phone, often by someone standing in a flooded kitchen or a sweltering attic. A pro makes sure your site loads in a couple of seconds, the call button is thumb-sized, and nothing breaks on a small screen. Slow or clunky sites silently bleed leads.

**Local SEO foundations.** Page titles, service-area pages, Google Business Profile alignment, schema markup — the boring technical work that helps you show up when someone searches "plumber near me." This is where DIY sites most often fall short, because the settings are buried and the rules change.

**Someone to call.** When something breaks or you need a landing page for a new service fast, you have a person. That reliability has real value when your website is how you eat.

## The Red Flags to Watch For (Both Sides)

**DIY traps:**

- Choosing a template because it looks pretty instead of because it fits a service business (giant photo sliders, no clear call button).
- Using low-resolution or stock photos when customers want to see *your* work and *your* team.
- Letting the site sit untouched for years.

**Done-for-you traps:**

- **You don't own anything.** Some providers hold your domain, your content, or your whole site hostage. If you leave, you start over. Always insist on owning your domain and getting admin access.
- **Rented websites.** "Free website with your $299/month package" usually means the site disappears when you cancel. Read the contract.
- **Pretty but useless.** A gorgeous site with no phone number above the fold and no local SEO is an expensive brochure.
- **No maintenance plan.** A site built and abandoned degrades — outdated plugins, broken forms, stale content.

## A Simple Decision Framework

Ask yourself three questions:

1. **What's my honest budget over the next 12 months?** Under ~$1,000 total points to DIY. A few thousand available points to done-for-you.
2. **Do I have 20–40 spare hours this quarter — and will I actually spend them on this?** Be honest. Most owners don't.
3. **How much does a single new job matter to me?** If one extra job a month pays for professional help, the math favors hiring it out sooner.

| Your situation | Likely better fit |
|---|---|
| Startup, tight cash, simple needs | DIY builder |
| Established, busy, site drives real revenue | Done-for-you |
| Somewhere in between | DIY now, pro refresh later — or a freelancer middle ground |

There's also a middle path many owners miss: hire a freelancer to set up the site properly on a platform you can edit yourself. You get a professional foundation and keep day-to-day control.

## Whichever Route You Pick, Get These Right

Whether you build it or buy it, these fundamentals decide whether the site works:

- Your phone number is visible at the top of every page, tap-to-call on mobile.
- Real photos of your work, your trucks, and your people.
- Your service area spelled out in plain text (search engines and customers both need it).
- Reviews or testimonials with real names and towns.
- A simple contact form that actually delivers — test it monthly.
- Fast load times and a clean mobile layout.

A modest site with these basics beats a flashy site without them every time.
