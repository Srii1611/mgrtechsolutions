Here's a customer you might be turning away without knowing it: the homeowner who is visually impaired and uses a screen reader to find a plumber. The older customer with arthritic hands who struggles with tiny buttons. The person who is colorblind trying to read your beige text on a tan background. Together, people with disabilities make up a substantial share of the population — and their families often make buying decisions with them in mind.

Web accessibility simply means building your website so people with disabilities can use it. It's good ethics, it's good business, and increasingly, it's a legal consideration. Here's what it means for a local business website, in practical terms.

## Why a Local Business Should Care

Three reasons, in ascending order of urgency for most owners:

**1. It's a bigger audience than you think.** Disabilities affecting web use — vision, hearing, motor control, cognition — touch a meaningful percentage of adults, and the share grows with age. For trades like HVAC, plumbing, and home services whose customer base skews toward older homeowners, an inaccessible website can quietly exclude a slice of your best customers.

**2. It overlaps with good SEO and usability.** The things that make a site accessible — clear structure, descriptive text, fast loading, readable fonts — are the same things search engines reward and all users appreciate. Accessibility work is rarely wasted work.

**3. Legal exposure is real.** Website accessibility lawsuits under the Americans with Disabilities Act have become common, and small businesses are not exempt from being targeted. Many settlements involve exactly the kinds of basic failures listed below. This isn't legal advice — but ignoring accessibility entirely is a risk you don't need to carry.

## The Four Principles, Translated

Accessibility standards (called WCAG, the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines) boil down to four ideas. Your site should be:

- **Perceivable:** Everyone can take in the information — text can be read by screen readers, images have descriptions, videos have captions.
- **Operable:** Everyone can navigate — the site works with a keyboard alone, buttons are big enough, nothing flashes in ways that cause harm.
- **Understandable:** The content and navigation make sense — clear language, predictable menus, helpful error messages on forms.
- **Robust:** The site works with the assistive technologies people actually use, now and as they evolve.

You don't need to memorize the standards. You need to handle the common issues below, which cover most of what matters for a typical local business site.

## The Most Common Accessibility Problems (and Fixes)

### Images Without Alt Text

Screen readers can't see your photos — they read the "alt text" behind each image. Every meaningful image on your site needs a short description: "Technician installing a furnace in a residential basement," not "image1.jpg." This takes minutes per image, helps blind visitors, and gives Google more context about your content. Purely decorative images can have empty alt text so screen readers skip them.

### Poor Color Contrast

Light gray text on white, white text on a pale blue banner — designers love subtle contrast; aging eyes do not. Text should have strong contrast against its background (free online contrast checkers will score any color pair in seconds). Also never convey meaning by color alone — if your quote form marks errors only by turning fields red, colorblind users won't know which field failed.

### Tiny Text and Tiny Tap Targets

Body text should be at least 16 pixels, and buttons and links large enough to tap with a thumb or click with a shaky hand. This is the same advice as mobile-first design — accessibility and mobile usability constantly overlap.

### Forms That Confuse Assistive Technology

Every form field needs a visible label ("Phone number"), not just placeholder text that vanishes when someone starts typing. Error messages should say what went wrong and how to fix it ("Please enter a 10-digit phone number") rather than just "invalid input." And the form must be completable with a keyboard alone — many users can't use a mouse.

### Keyboard Traps and Missing Focus

Try this on your own site: put the mouse aside and navigate using only the Tab key. You should be able to reach every menu item, link, and form field, and you should be able to *see* which element is selected (a visible outline or highlight). If Tab gets stuck somewhere — common with pop-ups and sliders — that's a keyboard trap, and it makes your site unusable for a whole class of visitors.

### Videos and Audio Without Alternatives

If you post videos — job walkthroughs, tips, ads — add captions. It serves deaf and hard-of-hearing visitors, and everyone watching with the sound off (which is most people on social media). Audio-only content needs a transcript.

### PDFs as a Substitute for Web Pages

A price list or brochure uploaded as a PDF is often completely unreadable to screen readers and miserable on phones. Put important content on real web pages; use PDFs only as optional downloads.

### Missing Page Titles and Headings Structure

Screen reader users navigate by headings the way sighted users scan a page. Use headings (H1, H2, H3) in logical order — one H1 per page, sections under H2s, subsections under H3s — rather than picking heading sizes for how they look. It's better for accessibility, better for SEO, and better for every reader.

## What About Accessibility Widgets and Overlays?

You've probably seen websites with a little accessibility icon that opens a toolbar, or vendors selling "instant ADA compliance" overlays. Be cautious. These tools make cosmetic adjustments (contrast toggles, text resizing) but cannot fix underlying code problems — and accessibility advocates and courts have been unimpressed by sites that rely on overlays instead of real fixes. Some lawsuits have specifically targeted sites *with* overlays installed. Treat them as a supplement at most, never as the solution.

## How to Check Your Site Today

A realistic, low-cost audit for a small business:

1. **The Tab test:** navigate your whole site with only a keyboard.
2. **The zoom test:** zoom your browser to 200% — is everything still usable?
3. **The phone test:** check contrast and readability in bright sunlight.
4. **Automated scan:** free tools like WAVE (wave.webaim.org) or the accessibility audit built into Chrome's Lighthouse will flag missing alt text, contrast failures, and structural issues in minutes.
5. **The real test:** if you can, watch someone who actually uses a screen reader or keyboard navigation try your site. Ten minutes of that teaches more than any report.

Automated tools catch maybe a third of real issues — they find what's measurable, not everything that matters. For a thorough review, hire an accessibility specialist, especially if you receive any legal complaint.

## Prioritize, Don't Panic

You don't need perfection overnight. Fix in this order:

1. **Contact paths first:** your phone number, quote form, and booking flow must be fully accessible — these are the revenue-critical elements.
2. **Core pages next:** homepage, services, about, contact.
3. **Everything else over time:** blog posts, galleries, old content, with alt text added as you go.

Add an accessibility statement page describing your commitment and how to report problems — it's a small gesture that signals good faith and gives frustrated users a path to reach you instead of a lawyer.
