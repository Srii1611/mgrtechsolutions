Drywall work has a feast-or-famine problem. You're slammed for two months, then the phone goes quiet and you're wondering whether to take that underpriced patch job just to keep the crew busy. The contractors who stay booked year-round aren't necessarily better hangers or finishers — they're the ones with a handful of simple marketing habits running in the background. This guide covers the lead sources that actually work for drywall businesses and how to set them up without turning marketing into a second job.

## Start With the Customers Who Already Trust You

The cheapest drywall lead you'll ever get is a past customer or someone they referred. Yet most contractors finish a job, cash the check, and never contact that customer again.

**Build a simple follow-up habit.** A week after each job, send a short text or email: thank them, ask if everything looks good after the paint went on, and mention you appreciate referrals. Thirty seconds of effort, and it plants a seed.

**Make referring easy.** Happy customers want to help but don't know how. Tell them exactly what to say: "If a neighbor mentions they need drywall work, just text them my number." Some contractors add a small thank-you — a $25 gift card per referral that turns into a job — and find it pays for itself many times over.

**Stay lightly in touch.** A twice-a-year email to past customers ("Booking up for spring remodels — want on the schedule?") keeps you top of mind. Old customers are your warmest audience; don't let them forget your name.

## Your Google Business Profile Is Your Best Salesperson

When a homeowner searches "drywall repair near me," the map results at the top of Google get the majority of the calls. Claiming and maintaining your Google Business Profile is free, and it's the single highest-return marketing task for a drywall contractor.

Do these things and you'll be ahead of most competitors:

- **Fill out everything.** Categories (Drywall Contractor, plus relevant secondaries), service area, hours, services list with descriptions.
- **Upload real photos monthly.** Before-and-after shots of smooth finishes, patches that vanished, texture matches. Blurry jobsite photos still beat no photos.
- **Collect reviews steadily.** Ask every satisfied customer right after the final walkthrough, while the "wow, you can't even tell" feeling is fresh. Send them the direct review link by text so it takes under a minute.
- **Respond to every review.** A short, professional reply to good and bad reviews alike signals you're an active, accountable business.

Ten genuine reviews with photos will usually out-pull a competitor with a nicer website and no reviews.

## Partner With the Trades Who Find Drywall Problems First

Drywall damage is usually discovered by someone else: a plumber opening a wall, a restoration company drying out a flood, a painter staring at cracked seams. Those trades are a referral goldmine because they hit your ideal customer at the exact moment of need.

Good referral partners for drywall contractors:

- **Plumbers and water damage restoration companies** — they create the holes you fix.
- **Painters** — customers often need repair or skim coating before paint, and painters who don't do drywall will happily hand it off.
- **Electricians and HVAC installers** — panel upgrades and new runs mean wall openings.
- **General contractors and remodelers** — steady subcontract work if you want it, especially smaller jobs bigger drywall companies ignore.
- **Property managers and realtors** — rental turnovers and pre-listing repairs repeat every month.

How to build these relationships: pick three trades, find two or three well-reviewed local companies in each, and introduce yourself — in person or by phone. Bring donuts to a plumber's shop. Offer to make *them* look good ("I'll get your customer's wall closed up fast so you can finish your job"). Reciprocity works: send them the referrals you can, and they'll return the favor.

## Show Off the Work (It's Your Best Content)

Drywall is visual. A perfectly blended patch or a Level 5 finish is exactly the kind of thing that makes a homeowner trust you before they've met you.

**Before-and-after photos are your marketing engine.** Get in the habit of snapping two photos on every job: the damage or rough board before, the finished wall after. Same angle, decent light. Store them in a folder on your phone and you've got months of marketing material.

**Where to use them:**

- Google Business Profile (as above)
- Your website's gallery or job stories
- Facebook and Instagram — a 30-second phone video of a skim coat or a texture match gets surprising reach in local community groups
- Nextdoor and local Facebook community groups, where "does anyone know a good drywall guy?" posts appear weekly

Don't overthink production quality. Authenticity beats polish for local services — people want to see real work, not stock photos.

## Fix Your Follow-Up Speed

Here's an uncomfortable truth: in many local markets, the first contractor to respond professionally wins the job, even at a higher price. Homeowners contacting three drywall companies often hire the one who called back within the hour.

Tighten your intake:

- **Answer or text back fast.** If you can't answer on a ladder, use a missed-call text-back feature or an answering service.
- **Give ballpark ranges early.** Many customers just want to know if their job is a $200 or $2,000 problem. A rough range with a photo-based quote process saves everyone time and builds trust.
- **Follow up on estimates.** Many contractors lose jobs not on price but on silence. A two-line follow-up three days after sending a quote ("Any questions on the estimate? Happy to walk through it") recovers a meaningful share of bids that would otherwise die quietly.

## Small Jobs Are Marketing, Not a Nuisance

The $150 doorknob-hole patch feels like a waste of a morning. Reframe it: every small repair is a paid audition. You're in the home, demonstrating professionalism, and that homeowner will need a basement finished, a garage hung, or a remodel patched someday — and they have neighbors.

Contractors who protect their reputation on tiny jobs consistently report that small repairs turn into their biggest projects. Leave a few business cards, do flawless cleanup, and treat the $150 job like a $15,000 one.

## What to Skip (For Now)

- **Expensive branding exercises.** Logos and truck wraps are nice, but they don't ring the phone like reviews and referrals do.
- **Broad paid ads before your foundation is set.** Google Ads can work for drywall, but if your profile has no reviews and your site has no photos, you're paying for clicks that don't convert.
- **Every social platform at once.** Pick one — usually Facebook or Instagram for this trade — and post your before-and-afters consistently.

## Quick Checklist

- [ ] Google Business Profile is claimed, complete, and gets new photos monthly
- [ ] Every happy customer gets asked for a review at final walkthrough, with a direct link
- [ ] I have a 1-week and 6-month follow-up habit for past customers
- [ ] I've introduced myself to at least 3 referral partners (plumber, painter, property manager, or restorer)
- [ ] I snap before-and-after photos on every job and post them somewhere weekly
- [ ] My call-back time on new inquiries is under an hour during work hours
- [ ] Every estimate gets a follow-up message within 3 days
- [ ] I treat small patch jobs as auditions for bigger work
