It's not glamorous, it never trends on marketing podcasts, and it's probably costing you customers right now. NAP — your business's Name, Address, and Phone number — is one of the most fundamental signals Google uses to decide which local businesses to trust and show in search results. When yours doesn't match across the internet, you bleed rankings and confuse customers. Here's what NAP consistency actually means, why it matters so much, and how to fix yours for good.

## What NAP Actually Is (And Isn't)

NAP is simply the core identifying information of your business:

- **Name:** Your exact legal or trading name as registered
- **Address:** Your physical business address (or service-area designation if you don't have a storefront)
- **Phone:** Your primary business phone number

It sounds simple because it is. The problem isn't understanding it — it's that this information lives in dozens of places online: your website, Google Business Profile, Facebook, Yelp, Angi, the BBB, industry directories, map apps. And it rarely matches perfectly across all of them.

Google's crawlers read all these sources to build confidence about who you are and where you are. When they find conflicting information — "Smith & Sons Plumbing" in one place, "Smith and Sons Plumbing LLC" in another, a different phone number somewhere else — confidence drops, and so do your rankings.

## Why Google Cares So Much

Google's entire business depends on showing searchers accurate, trustworthy results. When someone searches "plumber near me," Google needs to be confident that the businesses it shows are legitimate, reachable, and actually located where they claim.

Inconsistent NAP creates doubt. Think of it like checking references for a job applicant: if three references give you three different versions of the candidate's employment dates, you hesitate. Google does the same with your business information.

The specific ways inconsistency hurts:

- **Lower local rankings.** Google rewards businesses it can verify with higher placement in the map pack and local results.
- **Confused customers.** A prospect finds your Yelp listing with an old phone number, calls it, gets a disconnected tone, and assumes you're out of business.
- **Wasted citation value.** Each directory listing is a small trust signal. When they don't match, those signals don't accumulate — they fragment.

Many local SEO audits find NAP inconsistencies as a top-three ranking problem, especially for established businesses that have moved, rebranded, or changed phone systems over the years.

## Where Inconsistencies Come From

Most NAP problems aren't deliberate; they're the residue of business changes and sloppy data entry:

- **You moved** and updated your website but forgot the 40 directories still showing the old address
- **You changed phone systems** and the old number lingers on Facebook and a dozen directories
- **Your business name has variations** — "ABC Plumbing" vs. "ABC Plumbing & Heating" vs. "ABC Plumbing, Inc." — used interchangeably
- **Auto-generated listings** pulled incorrect data from an old source
- **A well-meaning employee** set up a profile with a personal cell number
- **Tracking numbers** used inconsistently, with different numbers on different platforms

One or two inconsistencies might not tank you. But they multiply. And once they spread to data aggregators — the companies that feed business information to hundreds of other sites — they replicate faster than you can manually fix them.

## The Golden Rule: One Canonical Version

The fix starts with deciding on one official version of your NAP and using it everywhere, character for character. This is your "canonical" NAP.

**Choose your business name format.** If your legal name is "Smith & Sons Plumbing, LLC," decide whether you'll use that everywhere or a shorter version like "Smith & Sons Plumbing." Then use it *exactly* that way — same ampersand, same capitalization, same punctuation, every time.

**Choose your address format.** Decide how you'll write your address and stick to it. "123 Main Street, Suite 200" vs. "123 Main St., Ste. 200" vs. "123 Main Street #200" — pick one. USPS standard abbreviations are fine; inconsistency is not.

**Choose your primary phone number.** Ideally, one number that rings to your business. If you use call-tracking numbers for specific campaigns, that's fine for ads — but your core listings (website, Google, major directories) should all show the same primary number.

Write your canonical NAP down. Share it with anyone who creates listings, updates your website, or manages your marketing. This is your source of truth.

## The Audit: Finding Every Place Your NAP Lives

You can't fix what you can't find. Here's how to hunt down your business information across the web:

**Start with the big ones:**

- Google Business Profile
- Your own website (header, footer, contact page)
- Facebook Business Page
- Yelp
- Angi
- Better Business Bureau
- Apple Maps
- Bing Places

**Then search for the stragglers.** Google these searches one at a time:

- "[Your exact business name]" (in quotes)
- "[Your phone number]"
- "[Your old phone number]" (if you've ever changed)
- "[Your old address]" (if you've ever moved)
- "[Common variations of your business name]"

Each search will surface listings you forgot about or never created. Make a spreadsheet: site name, what NAP it currently shows, whether it matches your canonical version.

**Check data aggregators.** Companies like Data Axle, Neustar Localeze, and Foursquare feed business data to hundreds of other sites. If your information is wrong there, it's wrong everywhere downstream. Most have a way to claim or correct your listing.

## The Fix: Systematic Cleanup

Once you have your audit list, work through it in priority order:

**Week 1: The essentials.** Fix your website, Google Business Profile, Facebook, Yelp, and Bing Places. These carry the most weight.

**Week 2–4: The major directories.** Work through Angi, BBB, Apple Maps, and your industry-specific directories (Houzz for remodelers, for example).

**Ongoing: The long tail.** The smaller directories and auto-generated listings can be fixed gradually. Many businesses find that fixing the top 15–20 listings resolves most of the ranking drag.

**How to actually claim and edit listings:** Most directories require you to claim your business profile first — usually by verifying a phone call, email, or postcard. It's tedious but necessary. For sites that won't let you edit directly, look for a "suggest an edit" or "report incorrect information" option.

**What about listings you can't control?** Some directories pull from aggregators and don't allow direct edits. Fix the aggregator source and the downstream listings often update within a few weeks to months.

## Special Situations

**If you've moved:** Update your address everywhere, starting with Google Business Profile and your website. Expect a temporary ranking fluctuation while Google re-verifies your location. The faster you clean up old addresses, the faster things stabilize.

**If you've rebranded:** Same process, plus an extra layer — search for the old name specifically and update or suppress every listing still showing it. Old brand names have a long half-life online.

**If you use call tracking:** It's fine to use tracking numbers in ads and on landing pages, but your website's main contact page, your Google Business Profile, and your core directory listings should all show your primary, permanent number.

**If you're a service-area business without a storefront:** Set your Google Business Profile as a service-area business and hide your home address. On your website and directories, use your service area ("Serving Springfield and surrounding areas") instead of a street address where possible — but stay consistent in how you describe it.

## Keeping It Consistent Going Forward

NAP consistency isn't a one-time project; it's an ongoing discipline:

- **Every new listing** gets created with your canonical NAP, no exceptions
- **When anything changes** — new number, new address, name tweak — update the core listings within a week
- **Audit annually.** Set a yearly reminder to re-run those Google searches and check the top 20 listings
- **One person owns it.** Make a specific person (or your marketing partner) responsible for NAP accuracy, so it doesn't fall through the cracks

## Quick Checklist

- [ ] Written down your one canonical Name, Address, Phone format
- [ ] Shared it with your team and any marketing partners
- [ ] Verified your website shows the canonical NAP in header, footer, and contact page
- [ ] Confirmed Google Business Profile matches exactly
- [ ] Checked and corrected Facebook, Yelp, Bing Places, Apple Maps, BBB, Angi
- [ ] Searched Google for your name, phone, old address, and name variations to find stray listings
- [ ] Created a tracking spreadsheet of every listing found and its status
- [ ] Claimed and corrected the top 15–20 listings
- [ ] Checked data aggregators for incorrect source data
- [ ] Committed to a yearly NAP audit

NAP consistency won't make for exciting dinner conversation. But it's a foundational block that makes everything else in local SEO work better. Get it right, keep it right, and stop leaking rankings over something so fixable.
