Plenty of local business blogs are graveyards — a dozen posts written with enthusiasm, read by no one, buried on page nine of the search results. The problem usually isn't effort; it's approach. Posts rank when they're built around what people actually search for, answer the question better than anyone else locally, and are easy for both readers and search engines to understand. Here's how to write posts that earn their keep.

## Step One: Write About Things People Actually Search

The most common reason a post never ranks is that nobody was ever looking for it. "Our Company Wins Community Award" might matter to you, but nobody searches it. Ranking starts with choosing questions your potential customers type into a search bar.

Your best source of topics is your own phone. Every question customers ask you is something other people are searching:

- **Symptom questions:** "Why is my furnace blowing cold air?" "Why does my shower drain smell?"
- **Cost questions:** "How much does a new roof cost?" "What does it cost to rewire a house?"
- **Comparison questions:** "Tank vs. tankless water heater" "Repair or replace a 15-year-old AC?"
- **Timing questions:** "How often should you clean gutters?" "When is the best time to seed a lawn?"
- **How-it-works questions:** "What happens during a sewer line inspection?"

Free keyword research tools can confirm how people phrase these and roughly how often they're searched, but don't overthink it — a real customer question, answered well, almost always has search demand behind it.

## Match What the Searcher Actually Wants

Search engines have gotten extremely good at judging whether a page satisfies the person who searched. Before writing, search your topic yourself and study what already ranks:

- **What's the format?** If the top results are step-by-step guides, a sales page won't rank. If they're short direct answers, a 3,000-word essay won't either.
- **What questions do the top results answer?** Your post needs to cover that ground — and then add something they lack.
- **What's the intent?** Someone searching "how to unclog a drain" wants instructions. Someone searching "drain cleaning cost" is comparing hiring options. Same topic, different pages. Match the intent or don't bother.

The goal isn't to copy what ranks — it's to understand what the searcher considers a satisfying answer, then deliver a better one.

## Structure the Post So Both Humans and Search Engines Get It

A rankable post follows a dependable shape:

### A headline that matches the search

If people search "why is my water heater making noise," a headline close to that question outperforms a clever one. Clarity beats creativity in search.

### A direct answer in the first paragraph

Don't bury the answer under three paragraphs of scene-setting. If someone asks why their water heater rumbles, tell them in the first two sentences — sediment buildup, most likely — then explain. Readers stay because you respected their time; search engines notice that readers stay.

### Descriptive headings that map the article

Break the post into sections with headings that say what each section delivers: "What causes the rumbling sound," "When it's a quick fix vs. a bigger problem," "What a professional flush costs." Headings help skimmers, and they help search engines understand the page's structure and coverage.

### Short paragraphs and plain language

Two to four sentences per paragraph. Write like you'd explain it to a customer in their kitchen. If a sentence needs a trade term, explain the term and move on.

### Specifics only you can provide

This is your edge over generic national content: your experience, your region, your photos. "In the older homes around here, we still see original galvanized drain lines" — that sentence is worth more than five paragraphs of generic advice, both to readers and to search engines trying to identify genuinely local, experienced content.

## The On-Page Basics That Still Matter

None of these are magic, but skipping them handicaps a good post:

- **Title tag and headline** that include the core phrase, kept natural
- **A compelling description** that earns the click when the post appears in results
- **A short, readable web address** — /blog/water-heater-rumbling-noise, not /blog/post-8472
- **Real images with descriptive file names and alt text** — especially photos of your actual work
- **Links between your posts and your service pages:** an article about water heater noises should link to your water heater repair page, and that service page should link back to helpful articles. This web of internal links helps search engines understand your expertise and moves readers toward becoming customers.
- **Fast loading on phones** — a brilliant post on a slow page still loses readers before they read a word.

## Write for One Reader, Not a Keyword Density

Old advice about repeating your keyword a precise number of times is dead, and following it makes writing worse. Modern search engines understand topics, synonyms, and context. If you thoroughly answer "why is my water heater making noise," you will naturally use every related phrase a searcher might type. Focus on being complete and clear, and the keyword coverage takes care of itself.

A good self-test: read the post aloud. If any sentence sounds like it was written for a robot, rewrite it for the human.

## The Uncomfortable Truth About Timing

New posts rarely rank overnight. For most local business sites, a well-written post takes weeks to months to find its footing — sometimes longer in competitive topics. This is normal, not failure.

What works over time:

- **Consistency:** one or two genuinely good posts a month beats ten rushed ones followed by silence
- **Coverage:** ten articles around your core services — symptoms, costs, comparisons, maintenance — build topical authority that lifts all of them
- **Refreshment:** revisit your best performers once or twice a year. Update prices, improve weak sections, add new photos. An updated post often outranks its original self.

## Promote Every Post (Lightly)

Publishing isn't the finish line. Each new post deserves:

- A share to your social channels with a one-line hook
- A spot in your next customer email, if you send one
- A link from any older, related posts on your site
- Your technicians and office staff knowing it exists — "we actually have an article about that" is a great line on a customer call

None of this is required for ranking, but early readers send positive signals, and every reader is a potential customer regardless of search position.

## Quick Checklist

- [ ] Topic is a real question customers ask, not company news
- [ ] Search results studied to match format and intent
- [ ] Headline mirrors how people actually search
- [ ] Direct answer given in the first paragraph
- [ ] Descriptive headings breaking the post into clear sections
- [ ] Short paragraphs, plain language, jargon explained
- [ ] Local detail and firsthand experience included
- [ ] Clean title, description, and readable web address
- [ ] Real photos with descriptive file names and alt text
- [ ] Links to related posts and the matching service page
- [ ] Page loads fast on a phone
- [ ] Post shared to social channels and linked from older content
- [ ] Top posts reviewed and refreshed once or twice a year
