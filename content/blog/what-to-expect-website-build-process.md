If you've never hired someone to build a website — or you did once and it went sideways — the process can feel like a black box. You pay a deposit, weeks pass, and eventually a website appears. A good build shouldn't feel that way. Here's what a professional website project actually looks like, step by step: how long it takes, what you'll be asked for, where projects stall, and how to come out the other side with a site that books jobs.

## How Long It Really Takes

For a typical local service business website — a homepage, service pages, contact and quote forms, maybe a gallery — expect **four to eight weeks** from kickoff to launch. Simpler sites can move faster; larger sites with lots of content take longer.

The honest secret of the industry: the timeline usually depends less on the builder than on you. The single biggest cause of delayed launches is waiting on content — photos, service descriptions, business details — from the business owner. More on how to prepare below.

## Phase 1: Discovery and Planning (Week 1)

A professional build starts with questions, not designs. Expect a kickoff conversation covering:

- **Your services and priorities:** which services make you the most money, which you want more of, which you'd rather not advertise
- **Your service area:** the towns you want to rank and be found in
- **Your customers:** who they are, what they worry about, why they choose you
- **Your competition:** who else shows up in local searches
- **Your goals:** more calls? More quote forms? Recruiting? All three?

From this, the builder produces a plan: the site structure (what pages exist and how they're organized), the key features, and a timeline with milestones. **Get the page list and scope in writing.** Most website disputes trace back to assumptions that were never written down.

## Phase 2: Content — Where You Come In (Weeks 1–3)

Here's the part that surprises most owners: a website needs words and photos, and someone has to provide them. Clarify early which of these arrangements you're in:

- **You provide everything:** you write the service descriptions and supply photos
- **The builder writes from your input:** you provide the raw material — notes, bullet points, a phone interview — and they turn it into pages
- **A hybrid:** they write, you review and correct for accuracy

Either way, you'll need to supply:

- **Photos of your real work, team, and trucks.** This is non-negotiable — stock photos of strangers undermine everything. Start a job-site photo habit now if you haven't.
- **Your service list with honest details:** what each service includes, rough pricing factors, common questions customers ask
- **Business specifics:** license numbers, insurance, certifications, warranties, years in business, service area
- **Reviews and testimonials** you'd like featured
- **Your logo files** and any brand colors, if you have established ones

Gathering this before the build starts can shave two weeks off the timeline. This is the single most useful thing you can do.

## Phase 3: Design and Build (Weeks 2–5)

Now the builder does their part. Typically you'll see:

1. **A homepage design first** — the look and feel: colors, fonts, layout, how your phone number and quote button appear. This is your main moment to react to the visual direction. Speak up honestly; "I don't love it but I can't say why" is worth saying, because changing direction now is cheap.
2. **Interior page layouts** — what a service page, gallery, and contact page will look like
3. **The full site built out** on a private preview link you can click through, on your phone as well as your computer

**Review on your phone first.** Most of your customers will visit on mobile, and a site that looks great on a designer's big monitor can be clumsy on a small screen.

### How to give feedback that helps

- Be specific: "the headline should mention our town" beats "make it pop"
- Consolidate feedback into one round of notes per review, from one decision-maker — feedback by committee, drip-fed over weeks, stalls projects like nothing else
- Separate "must fix" from "personal taste" — your site is for your customers, and sometimes the design you like least converts best

## Phase 4: Testing and Launch (Weeks 5–6)

Before launch, a professional build includes a testing pass. You should personally verify:

- **The phone number is correct and tappable** on mobile (you'd be amazed how often this is wrong)
- **Forms actually send** — submit a test request and confirm it arrives and someone gets notified
- **Every page loads quickly** on a phone connection
- **Business name, address, and phone match your listings** elsewhere exactly
- **No placeholder text** hiding anywhere ("lorem ipsum" has embarrassed many a launch)

Then the site goes live on your domain. Confirm in writing that **you own the domain, you own the content, and you have full admin access** — this protects you if the relationship ever ends.

## Phase 5: After Launch — the Part Nobody Mentions

A website launch is a starting line, not a finish. Plan for:

- **Ongoing maintenance:** software updates, backups, security, small fixes. Ask up front what this costs monthly and what's included.
- **Small changes:** who updates your hours, adds a service, or swaps photos, and at what cost or turnaround time?
- **Growth:** adding blog posts, new service pages, and location pages over time — the compounding work that builds search visibility
- **Realistic expectations:** a new site doesn't rank the day it launches. Local search visibility typically builds over months. If anyone promises instant first-page rankings, be skeptical.

## How to Be a Great Client (and Get a Better Site)

The owners who get the best results from a build tend to:

- Respond to questions and review requests within a day or two
- Provide real photos and honest input early
- Make one person the decision-maker
- Trust the builder on conversion and search matters — you hired expertise; use it
- Stay engaged after launch instead of treating the site as done

## Quick Checklist

- [ ] Four to eight week timeline expected for a typical service business site
- [ ] Page list, scope, and milestones confirmed in writing
- [ ] Content responsibilities agreed: who writes, who reviews
- [ ] Real photos of work, team, and trucks gathered before kickoff
- [ ] License, insurance, warranty, and service-area details compiled
- [ ] Homepage design reviewed before full build-out
- [ ] Preview tested on your phone, not just a computer
- [ ] Feedback consolidated from one decision-maker per round
- [ ] Phone number, forms, and business details personally tested before launch
- [ ] Domain ownership, content ownership, and admin access confirmed in writing
- [ ] Monthly maintenance plan and costs understood
- [ ] Post-launch content plan for services, locations, and articles
