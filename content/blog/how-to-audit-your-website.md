You don't need to be technical to know whether your website is working. Most of what makes a service business website succeed or fail is visible to anyone willing to spend an hour clicking through it with fresh eyes. This step-by-step audit walks you through exactly what to check, what "good" looks like, and what to fix first — no developer required.

## Before You Start: Set Up Your Audit

Grab a notebook or open a document with three columns: **What I found**, **Priority (high/med/low)**, and **Who fixes it** (you, your web person, your office manager). Then gather two things:

- Your smartphone (most of your customers are on phones — audit like a customer)
- A computer with an incognito/private browser window (so you see the site like a stranger, not a logged-in owner)

Plan to check everything twice: once on mobile, once on desktop. Score each section below pass/fail and move on.

## Step 1: The 5-Second First Impression Test

Open your homepage. Within five seconds, can a stranger answer:

1. What do you do?
2. Where do you do it?
3. How do they contact you?

If any answer requires scrolling or guessing, that's a fail. Your headline should name your service and your area ("Custom Kitchen Cabinets in Columbus"), not a vague slogan ("Quality You Can Trust"). Your phone number should be visible at the top without scrolling — and tappable on mobile.

**Also check:** Is the main photo of *your* work, or a generic stock image anyone could buy? Real photos pass; stock is a yellow flag.

## Step 2: The Obvious Trust Signals

Homeowners hiring someone to work in their house look for proof you're legitimate and current:

- **Reviews or testimonials** visible on the homepage
- **License/insurance info** mentioned somewhere obvious
- **Real photos** of your team, trucks, or finished work
- **A recent sign of life** — a project photo or update from this year, not 2019
- **The padlock (HTTPS)** in the address bar. If browsers say "Not Secure," jump to high priority

Stale content quietly kills trust. If your site says "Serving the area since 2009" but the newest photo looks a decade old, visitors assume you may be out of business.

## Step 3: Mobile Experience (Do This First)

Over half your visitors — often far more for emergency services — are on phones. On your smartphone, check:

- **Text size:** Readable without pinching or zooming?
- **Buttons:** Big enough to tap with a thumb? Is the phone number click-to-call?
- **Forms:** Can you fill out your own contact form one-handed without frustration? Actually try it.
- **Pop-ups:** Does anything cover the screen and refuse to close on mobile?
- **Menu:** Does navigation open and work on a small screen?

A site that's beautiful on desktop but clumsy on a phone is failing most of its visitors.

## Step 4: Speed Test

Slow sites bleed leads. People hit the back button within a few seconds of a blank screen.

1. Go to Google PageSpeed Insights (free) and enter your homepage URL.
2. Run it for both mobile and desktop.
3. Note the score: above 70 is fine for most small sites; below 50 needs attention.

You don't need to understand the technical details. The most common cause of slow contractor sites is **oversized photos** — images uploaded straight from a phone or camera at full size. If your score is poor, ask your web person about compressing images and enabling caching. Those two fixes solve most small-business speed problems.

**Quick manual test:** Time how long your homepage takes to load on your phone using cellular data (not Wi-Fi). If it's more than about 3 seconds to something usable, flag it.

## Step 5: Content That Answers Questions

Click through your main pages and ask:

- **Does each core service have its own page?** "Services" as one lump page is weak. Separate pages for each service (water heater repair, drain cleaning, repiping) rank better and convert better.
- **Does the site say WHERE you work?** Your city, service area, or the towns you cover should appear naturally throughout — homepage, service pages, footer.
- **Is anything obviously out of date?** Old prices, discontinued services, past promotions, a "team" page listing people who left.
- **Is the writing about the customer or about you?** Pages that open with the customer's problem ("Dealing with a furnace that won't heat?") outperform pages that open with company history.

## Step 6: Contact Points and Lead Capture

This is where audits get uncomfortable — many sites fail here.

- **Call your own number** during business hours. How many rings? Who answers? What does the voicemail say?
- **Submit your own contact form.** Did you get a confirmation? How long until someone responded? Time it.
- **Check the form email.** Test submissions sometimes go to an old employee's inbox or a spam folder.
- **Look for friction:** Required phone-number fields, CAPTCHAs that fight back, forms asking ten questions before saying hello.

Many owners are shocked to discover a broken form or an unanswered inbox that's been leaking leads for months.

## Step 7: Can Google Find and Understand You?

Do a few plain-English searches in an incognito window:

- Search **your business name** — do you appear with correct info?
- Search **"[your service] [your city]"** — do you appear on page one? In the map listings?
- Search **"site:yourdomain.com"** — roughly how many pages does Google have indexed?

Then check page titles: hover over your browser tab on key pages. The title should describe the page and location ("Water Heater Repair | Springfield, IL"), not just "Home" or your company name. Titles are what show in search results — they're your first ad copy.

Also verify your **Google Business Profile** link points to your website, and the name/address/phone on your site matches your profile exactly.

## Step 8: The Competitive Gut Check

Pick two competitors who rank above you and spend five minutes on each site:

- What do they show that you don't? (Financing info? Instant booking? More photos? Review counts?)
- How fast do their sites load compared to yours?
- What does their call to action look like?

You're not copying — you're calibrating what customers see when they comparison-shop, because they absolutely are comparison-shopping.

## Scoring Your Audit: What to Fix First

Tally your fails and prioritize like this:

| Priority | Issue type | Why first |
|---|---|---|
| High | Broken forms, wrong phone number, no HTTPS, mobile failures | Actively losing leads today |
| High | Missing service-area info, no reviews visible | Blocking trust and rankings |
| Medium | Slow speed, stock photos, stale content | Leaking conversions steadily |
| Medium | Weak page titles, lumped-together services | Limiting search visibility |
| Low | Design polish, blog gaps, fancy features | Nice once the rest works |

Fix the top row this week — most are fast and cheap. Schedule medium items over the next month.

## Make It a Habit, Not a Project

A one-time audit decays. Put a 20-minute mini-audit on your calendar quarterly: test the form, check mobile, scan for outdated info, run one speed test. Websites don't fail all at once — they drift. Quarterly checks catch the drift before it costs you jobs.

## Quick Checklist

- [ ] Homepage passes the 5-second test: what, where, how to contact
- [ ] Phone number visible and tappable on mobile
- [ ] HTTPS padlock showing on every page
- [ ] Site loads in ~3 seconds on a phone over cellular
- [ ] Every core service has its own page
- [ ] Your city/service area appears on key pages
- [ ] Submitted your own contact form and confirmed it works
- [ ] Called your own number and rated the experience
- [ ] Page titles describe the service and location
- [ ] Reviews, real photos, and a recent update visible
- [ ] Site info matches your Google Business Profile exactly
- [ ] Logged every fail with a priority and an owner
- [ ] Scheduled a quarterly 20-minute mini-audit

An hour, a notebook, and honest eyes — that's the whole audit. Most owners find three to five fixable problems costing them real leads, and most fixes take days, not months.
